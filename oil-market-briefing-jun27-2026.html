Top Story

Weekend Flash — Bessent's Sanctions Waiver Removes the Last Major Legal Barrier: Brent Tests the February Pre-War Baseline

US Treasury Issues 60-Day Sanctions Waiver on Iranian Oil Purchases as VP Vance Arrives in Switzerland — Brent Falls to $72.86, Its Lowest Since the Day Before the War Began

The weekend delivered the development that crude traders had been watching for since the June 17 MOU but had not fully priced: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced Saturday a 60-day general sanctions waiver covering foreign purchases of Iranian crude oil, effectively removing the threat of US secondary sanctions from Iranian-origin barrels for the next two months. The waiver — running through August 21 — is the most direct intervention yet to formally accelerate Iran's crude export recovery, converting a ceasefire that was diplomatic into a policy framework that is legally operative for buyers. Brent crude fell to $72.86 (−2.1%) in weekend trading, the lowest level since February 27, 2026, the last trading day before the conflict began. WTI dipped to $70.14 (−1.6%), pressing on the $70 psychological threshold that has functioned as the market's near-term floor. Separately, Vice President JD Vance landed in Geneva to lead the second phase of Iran peace negotiations, signaling that the White House views a permanent agreement as achievable within weeks.

The significance of the Treasury waiver is structural, not merely symbolic. Iranian crude exports had been constrained not only by logistics but by the legal risk of secondary-sanctions exposure for buyers — particularly Chinese independent refineries ('teapots'), Indian state refiners, and South Korean processors. With the waiver formally in place, those buyers can expand Iranian crude purchases without Treasury exposure, potentially unlocking approximately 1 million barrels per day of incremental Iranian supply within the 60-day window. Iran's pre-war export capacity ran approximately 1.7 mb/d; the country's fields have been partially restarting since the ceasefire, and with the legal barrier removed, the pace of restart is now a logistics question, not a regulatory one. The 60-day clock also concentrates the market's attention on what comes after August 21: whether the waiver is extended, converted into a permanent license, or lapses as Iran's nuclear inspection dispute with the IAEA remains unresolved.

Hormuz transit data for June 26 confirmed more than 70 vessel transits — double the prior day — as ship operators recalibrate their routing economics in response to improving conditions and declining war-risk insurance premiums. Estimated flows are approaching 13 mb/d, still well below the pre-war 20 mb/d baseline but recovering rapidly. Saudi Aramco's Ras Tanura terminal — which resumed operations for the first time since early March just days ago — has now logged multiple VLCC loadings at the Ju'aymah offshore buoys. The physical supply return is no longer a projection; it is visible in AIS shipping data and terminal loading records. The market's attention has shifted almost entirely to demand: with supply returning faster than most base-case models projected, the central question for crude in July and August is whether demand — suppressed by elevated prices since March — can absorb the returning barrels at anything meaningful above $65–$70/bbl.

Vance in Geneva is the clearest signal yet that the administration views a treaty as weeks, not months, away.

Hormuz Records 70+ Vessel Transits on June 26 — Second Consecutive Daily Surge as Flows Approach 13 mb/d

Strait of Hormuz transit data for June 26 confirmed more than 70 vessel transits — double the prior day's count — according to AIS shipping analytics. Estimated flows reached approximately 13 mb/d, recovering rapidly from the near-zero transit levels during the peak conflict period but still materially below the pre-war 20 mb/d baseline. The surge reflects simultaneous forces: the clearing of the backlog of stranded tankers and the re-routing of Asia-bound crude from longer Cape of Good Hope corridors back through the strait. War-risk insurance premiums remain elevated but are declining, with VLCC daily earnings still approximately $400,000 — well above pre-conflict norms — as mine-clearance operations continue. The critical question flagged by ING analysts last week: once the backlog clears, the structural transit rate — not the surge from rerouted backlogs — will set the true new equilibrium. That rate becomes visible in the July 2 EIA import data.

Oman Cargo Strike Fallout: Ceasefire Holds, Brent Spike Fully Reversed — Mine Risk and Non-State Actors Remain Live Factors

Friday's cargo ship strike near Oman — which briefly sent Brent spiking roughly 2% before the move was entirely reversed within hours — drew a clear line under the new market psychology: geopolitical incidents without a direct crude supply implication are now treated as noise, not signal. Iran denied involvement; the vessel was not a crude carrier; the ceasefire framework was affirmed by both sides. But the incident is a useful prompt for the risk register that no analyst should dismiss. Iranian mines remain in the Strait of Hormuz, the US mine-clearance operation is months from completion, and the Arabian Sea security environment includes non-state actors with unclear motivations. Friday's price response reflects current market consensus that these risks are manageable and contained. A vessel strike on a crude tanker, or any incident with a verifiable Iranian military fingerprint, would behave very differently — and there is no premium left in the price to absorb it.

The Guru's Verdict — Saturday, June 27, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Saturday morning's headline — Bessent's 60-day sanctions waiver on Iranian oil — is the single most significant policy development since the June 17 MOU signing. Not because it was unexpected; the market had been pricing a high probability of some form of sanctions relief. But because it converts a probability into a legal fact, and legal facts move institutional buyers in a way that probabilities don't. Chinese teapots that were running risk-weighted models on Iranian crude costs can now purchase at spot without the legal uncertainty discount. Indian state refiners who had been cautiously accumulating small cargoes can go to full scale. The 60-day window is real, and buyers will move inside it. The last major non-physical barrier to Iranian crude's return has been removed.

My read on this weekend: The sell-off is rational, but the pace matters. Brent at $72.86 is already inside the range where Saudi Arabia's fiscal arithmetic becomes uncomfortable — not crisis-level, but uncomfortable enough to sharpen minds in Riyadh. And the August 21 expiry creates a natural counter-pressure: buyers who want Iranian crude at the lowest possible price will front-load purchases now, while Iran itself has an incentive to pace exports and avoid flooding the market. The practical result is that the decline from here is likely to be more gradual than the drop from $144 to $74 was. That parabolic move was a single-factor unwind — the war premium. The next leg, from the mid-$70s toward a post-war equilibrium in the $65–$70 range, requires actual physical barrels arriving and OPEC+ choosing not to defend the floor. Both are probable; neither is instantaneous.

The bigger picture: The risk matrix has shifted asymmetrically. The downside path — Treasury waiver, Hormuz flowing, Ras Tanura loading, Iranian barrels returning — is clearly the base case and is being priced methodically. But the tail risks are not zero: an IAEA-driven breakdown in Vance's Geneva talks could cause the August 21 waiver to lapse; a mine incident in Hormuz involving a crude tanker would re-rate transit risk in a single session; and a Russian diesel export ban — which Deputy PM Novak has explicitly signaled as under active consideration — would spike the refined-products complex without touching the crude supply story. The market is buying the base case and selling the tails. That's rational. It's also the positioning that snaps back hardest if any tail risk materializes — and with no war premium left as a shock absorber, the reversal would be sharp and fast.

My call: Brent $68–$74 over the next two weeks, with the July 2 EIA report as the first major test. If the week-ending June 26 data shows a build of 4+ million barrels — the first evidence of Gulf crude arriving at US shores in quantity — the $70 WTI support is the next live test. I'd set the interim floor at $68–$70 Brent, anchored by OPEC+ fiscal breakeven logic and the near-certainty of a Saudi signal before that level breaks in sustained fashion. The ceiling, absent a diplomatic shock, is $75–$77 — the bottom of last week's range. Position for the gradual decline. Hedge the tail. Watch August 21 as intensely as you watched June 19.
