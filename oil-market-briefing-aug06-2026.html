Top Story

The Final Gate — Geographic Coordinates Agreed, Washington's Legal Text Review Holds the Line

Iran and Oman Confirm Hormuz Corridor Route — Rubio Says 'Progress, Not Finality' as Brent Holds Below $80 for Second Straight Session

The Hormuz corridor deal crossed a critical technical threshold on Thursday as Iran and Oman announced they had agreed on the precise geographic coordinates for the new transit route through the strait — converting the framework from a diplomatic concept into an operational specification. Under the emerging 60-day interim arrangement, commercial ships would enter the Persian Gulf via an Iranian-controlled inbound lane and exit via an Omani-controlled outbound lane, with no transit fees during the provisional period. The routing agreement represents the most concrete single step since Qatar confirmed a draft proposal on Wednesday, and traders interpreted it as the last technical precondition to a formal signing. Brent crude dipped a further 0.44% to $79.10 per barrel, while WTI declined 0.52% to $74.83 — continuing to hold in the deal-pricing band that has prevailed since Monday's opening session began erasing the prior week's war-premium reconstruction.

Washington's posture remained a step behind the operational progress. Secretary of State Marco Rubio offered "progress, but not finality," reiterating the core American sticking point: the US refuses to accept any arrangement that leaves Iran with permanent control over, or tolling rights on, the waterway. Iran continues to insist that its sovereign rights over its territorial waters must be explicitly recognized in the text of any corridor agreement. The gap is narrow — both sides are bargaining over the precise legal framing of routing authority, not over whether the corridor exists — but the absence of a US sign-off means Thursday's session is technically a continuation trade rather than a post-signing clearance. President Trump's statement that a deal could come "as early as today" had not materialized into a joint communiqué before the European close, and traders have learned to treat deal-window language with at least one session of skepticism before pricing it as confirmed.

The session carried structural significance beyond the immediate diplomatic timeline. OPEC's July output survey, published by Bloomberg, showed total production climbed by 1.16 million barrels per day to 19.44 mb/d — the largest monthly gain since the Hormuz disruption began in late February — led by Saudi Arabia (+390,000 b/d), Iraq (+460,000 b/d via southern port recovery), and Kuwait (+360,000 b/d). Yet the group remains several million barrels below pre-war levels, meaning a Hormuz corridor would arrive into a market that is already recovering incrementally from the supply side. The IEA's July Oil Market Report cuts across both supply and demand simultaneously: it projects global oil demand will contract by approximately 1 million barrels per day year-on-year in 2026 — the first annual demand decline since the pandemic year of 2020 — as elevated fuel prices and economic disruption from the Iran war have destroyed consumption at a rate that the supply shock alone cannot fully price away. Corridor or not, the fundamental picture has shifted: this is no longer a simple supply-shortage story.

OPEC+ / Supply

OPEC July Output Rises 1.16 mb/d to 19.44 mb/d — Largest Monthly Gain Since Hormuz Closure; Iraq's Southern Exports Jump 37%

Bloomberg's monthly OPEC output survey for July 2026 found total production climbed 1.16 million barrels per day to 19.44 mb/d — the largest single-month gain since the Hormuz disruption began in late February. Iraq's southern port exports jumped 37%; Kuwait reached its highest output level since the conflict started; Saudi Arabia added 390,000 b/d. The group has now approved a fifth consecutive monthly quota increase of 188,000 b/d for August. Despite the recovery, OPEC+ remains several million barrels below pre-war levels, with physical shipping constraints — not quota — governing the pace of restoration. A working Hormuz corridor could accelerate the recovery trajectory significantly, arriving into a market that has already partially adjusted from the supply side.

Earnings / Majors

Aramco Profit Jumps 33% in Q2 to $32.7bn — CEO Nasser Warns War 'Continues to Aggravate the Biggest Supply Shock in History'

Saudi Aramco reported Q2 2026 net income of approximately $32.69 billion, up 33% year-on-year, driven by elevated crude and refined product prices throughout the quarter. CEO Amin Nasser described the Iran war as continuing to 'aggravate the biggest supply shock in history,' noting the world has lost access to more than 2.6 billion barrels of redirected oil since the conflict began. Aramco highlighted its 1,200-km East-West pipeline to the Red Sea as the critical bypass infrastructure enabling it to maintain elevated exports. The results accompanied exceptional earnings across Western majors: ExxonMobil ($14.5bn — highest in four years), Chevron ($12bn — highest in six years), Shell (~$10bn), TotalEnergies (+67%), and BP ($5.73bn) all reported multi-year-high quarterly profits amid the war-premium environment.

Demand / IEA

IEA: 2026 Global Oil Demand Heading for First Annual Decline Since COVID as War Destroys Consumption

The IEA's July 2026 Oil Market Report projected global oil demand will fall by approximately 1 million barrels per day year-on-year in 2026 — the first annual contraction since the pandemic-impacted year of 2020. The agency attributed the demand destruction directly to elevated fuel prices and economic disruption caused by the Iran war and Hormuz closure: US gasoline averaged above $4 per gallon, with similar spikes in Europe and Asia driving consumers and industries to curtail fuel consumption at a pace exceeding normal price elasticity. The IEA sees a potential supply surplus forming by end-2026 if Hormuz flows gradually recover — a scenario with significant bearish implications for the H2 2026 price path even before the corridor has moved a single tanker.

The Guru's Verdict — Thursday, August 6, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Thursday's session is the corridor deal's most important test so far — not because of what happened, but because of what did not. The oil market absorbed a full day of 'progress but not finality' at $79.10 without panic in either direction. That price stability is itself a signal. If the market genuinely doubted the corridor, it would have retraced more sharply toward $83–85; instead, it held the deal-pricing band with Asian buying providing the floor. The market has made a collective judgment that the geographic coordinates agreement means the corridor will happen — and that Washington's text review is a procedural gate, not a substantive barrier. I share that read, with one important qualification: the specific language around Iranian sovereign rights has derailed agreements before, and Rubio's phrasing was measured rather than dismissive.

My read on today: We are living in the briefest gap between 'done operationally' and 'signed officially.' Brent at $79.10 is pricing approximately an 80% probability of a formal corridor agreement by Friday's Asia open. The remaining 20% reflects the Rubio sticking point — but Rubio's language has historically been his opening bid in a final negotiation, not his closing position. The IEA demand data, however, is the emerging story beneath the diplomatic noise. A 1 mb/d demand contraction in 2026 means that even a partial corridor recovery — say, 3–4 mb/d of restored Gulf flow — arrives into a market that is structurally smaller than it was twelve months ago. The post-corridor price floor is not $80. It may be closer to $68–72 if the demand destruction proves sticky — and the pre-corridor EIA build suggests softening was already underway.

The bigger picture: The OPEC July output recovery — +1.16 mb/d in a single month, before a single corridor barrel flows — tells us that Gulf producers have been working around the Hormuz closure more aggressively than markets fully credited: dark tankers, pipeline diversions, port rehabilitation. When the corridor opens, it does not supplement those workarounds; it replaces them and adds incremental volume on top. That supply-side picture is structurally more bearish than the 'corridor opens, problem solved' narrative implies. The demand side is also weaker, consumption having been destroyed at a pace not seen since the pandemic. Both vectors point lower over the medium term. The Brent chart today looks like stability. The fundamental sheet looks like pressure building toward a downside re-rating.

My call: Brent $76–$82 today and through the weekend, biased toward the lower bound on corridor signing and toward $82+ only if Rubio's text review stalls beyond 48 hours. The week-to-date decline of roughly 10% from Monday's open has fully priced a working corridor. The next trade is not whether the deal happens — it is how many barrels actually move and at what pace. Watch the tanker transit count on Kpler and Vortexa. That number, not any press conference, is what sets the H2 2026 price.
