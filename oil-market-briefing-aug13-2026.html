Six-Day Rally Breaks: EIA's Record Build Digests and Houthis Open a Second Front — Both Bab el-Mandeb and Hormuz Now Under Active Interdiction

Brent Snaps Six-Session Streak at $87.92 as +17.4M-Barrel EIA Shock Digests — Houthi's First Fatal Red Sea Strike in a Year Complicates the Picture

Brent crude snapped a six-session winning streak on Thursday, falling to ~$87.92/bbl (−1.19%) as the market finally fully digested Wednesday's bombshell EIA weekly petroleum status report: a +17.4 million barrel crude build — the second-largest single-week inventory surge on record — driven primarily by a dramatic slump in U.S. crude exports to 3.06 million barrels per day, the lowest level since November 2025. The build dwarfed analyst expectations for a 1.4-million-barrel draw and lifted commercial crude stocks to 424.4 million barrels, their highest since June 5. The mechanism is the Hormuz paradox: the strait closure that supports prices geopolitically is simultaneously trapping U.S. crude onshore, building domestic inventories at a historic pace as export routes are disrupted. Wednesday's session absorbed the data with only modest losses before recovering; by Thursday, the tape could no longer look away.

Even as the bearish inventory data weighed, a new supply-chain shock emerged to set a floor. Houthi forces confirmed a deadly missile attack on the Egyptian-owned, Tanzania-flagged cargo ship Tihamah in the Bab el-Mandeb strait — killing six crew members, the first fatal strike in the waterway in more than a year. The attack marks a significant tactical escalation: with the Strait of Hormuz already restricted by Iran to roughly 6–10 vessel transits per day (versus ~130 pre-war), the Houthis appear to be opening a southern front to squeeze the Saudi Red Sea export route that had been absorbing some of the Hormuz disruption via the East-West pipeline to Yanbu. Bab el-Mandeb throughput — which had already been halved before Tuesday's attack — now faces another potential step down. The dual-chokepoint scenario, both Bab el-Mandeb and Hormuz simultaneously under active interdiction, is the most bearish structural oil supply narrative in decades.

On the diplomatic front, cautiously encouraging signals also landed Thursday. Qatar's Ministry of Foreign Affairs confirmed Oman-Iran Hormuz negotiations are at an 'advanced stage,' with route coordinates for approved shipping lanes reportedly agreed between the parties. Secretary of State Rubio acknowledged U.S. involvement in the mediation and said 'progress has been made' — but added there is 'not finality' yet. A Bloomberg Opinion piece argued pointedly that 'for the oil market, the Strait of Hormuz isn't closed,' noting that EIA data shows roughly 4.9 million bpd still flowed through the strait in Q2 2026, against 21.6 million bpd pre-war — a 77% reduction but not a complete shutoff. The net effect: Brent is caught between a historic domestic crude build that argues for further retreat and a worsening dual-chokepoint supply risk that argues for a floor. Thursday's $87–$88 holding level reflects exactly that standoff.

EIA Data

EIA: +17.4M Barrel Crude Build — Second-Largest in History as Hormuz Export Slump Traps U.S. Oil Onshore

The EIA's weekly petroleum status report, released Wednesday August 12, delivered a +17.4 million barrel crude build for the week ending August 7 — the second-largest single-week inventory increase on record — as U.S. crude exports plummeted to 3.06 million barrels per day, the lowest since November 2025. Analysts had expected a 1.4-million-barrel draw; the actual print followed the API's Tuesday-evening estimate of +9.1 million barrels. Commercial crude stocks rose to 424.4 million barrels — their highest since June 5 — while refinery utilization ran at a robust 96.2%. The core paradox: the Hormuz closure that supports prices geopolitically is simultaneously blocking U.S. export routes, building domestic inventories at a record pace. Gasoline stocks fell 1.0 million barrels (now 6% below the five-year average); distillate fuel fell just 0.1 million barrels (still 12% below average, its own structural support for refinery margins).

Supply Risk

Houthis Kill Six on Red Sea Cargo Ship Tihamah — First Fatal Bab el-Mandeb Strike in a Year Opens a Second Chokepoint

Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi forces killed six crew members aboard the Egyptian-owned, Tanzania-flagged cargo ship Tihamah in the Bab el-Mandeb strait on Tuesday — the first fatal strike in the waterway in more than a year and a significant escalation from the Houthis' recently announced Red Sea blockade. The attack compounds the Hormuz closure in a structurally alarming way: Saudi Arabia had been partially offsetting the Iran-restricted Hormuz route by re-routing crude via its East-West pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu — a route that transits Bab el-Mandeb. With Bab el-Mandeb throughput already halved before the attack, a sustained Houthi interdiction of the Red Sea would foreclose the only meaningful alternative to Hormuz for Gulf producers. The desk flags this as the most under-priced tail risk in the current market: two chokepoints, both under active attack simultaneously.

Qatar: Oman-Iran Hormuz Talks in 'Advanced Stage' — Route Coordinates Agreed; Rubio Says 'Progress Made, Not Finality'

Qatar's Ministry of Foreign Affairs confirmed Wednesday that Oman-Iran negotiations over Strait of Hormuz transit routes have reached an 'advanced stage,' with the coordinates of approved shipping lanes reportedly agreed between the parties. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, in remarks to reporters, confirmed U.S. involvement in the mediation and said 'progress has been made' — but added there is 'not finality' and expressed hope a deal will materialize 'very shortly.' Iran's Foreign Ministry simultaneously signaled that an Oman-mediated framework alone would be insufficient without the U.S. meeting a full list of conditions, including ending its naval blockade of Iranian ports, withdrawing military forces from Iran's vicinity, and permanently ending the war. The gap between 'advanced-stage talks' and a framework that satisfies Iran's public conditions remains significant, but the diplomatic language is the most constructive seen in over a week.

Analysis

Bloomberg Opinion: 'The Strait of Hormuz Isn't Closed' — 4.9M bpd Still Flowing; What Is the Market Actually Pricing?

A Bloomberg Opinion piece published Thursday argues the oil market's binary framing of a 'closed' Hormuz strait overstates the disruption: EIA data shows 4.9 million barrels per day still transited the strait in Q2 2026, against 21.6 million bpd in Q4 2025 pre-war — a 77% decline, not a shutoff. The piece notes that Brent's range of $69–$105 since the conflict began reflects a market wrestling with a partial, variable closure rather than a clean binary state. The argument carries a bearish implication: if the market has overpriced the closure by treating it as total rather than severe-but-partial, any clarity on actual flow volumes — or the Oman deal formalizing partial access — could trigger meaningful repricing lower. The bulls counter that Thursday's Houthi Bab el-Mandeb escalation changes the calculus entirely: if the Red Sea alternative route is also closing, it no longer matters that Hormuz was 'only' 77% disrupted.

IEA: Global Oil Deficit to Hit 1.8M bpd This Quarter — Demand Outlook Cut 510K bpd as High Prices Bite

The International Energy Agency's August Oil Market Report delivered a double-sided verdict on the crisis: a global oil supply shortfall of 1.8 million barrels per day in Q3 2026 — more than double its prior projection — as the U.S.-Iran conflict dragged on, while simultaneously cutting its 2026 global demand outlook by 510,000 bpd as elevated fuel prices suppress consumption. Global oil supply is forecast to fall 4.3 mb/d in 2026 to 102 mb/d. The agency, which has characterized the Hormuz closure as the 'largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market,' noted that China's strategic reserve draws (1.7 mb/d) and record U.S. production have been the primary buffers. The IEA also flagged that a Caroline Bezengi sanctioned Russian crude tanker ran aground near the Omani coast, producing an oil spill spreading across more than 2,000 sq km — an environmental crisis with secondary maritime traffic implications for an already-stressed waterway.

The Guru's Verdict — Thursday, August 13, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Two forces that should push in opposite directions are instead creating a standoff. The EIA's +17.4 million barrel crude build — the second-largest in history — is the starkest domestic supply-side data point in years, and it is unambiguously bearish for U.S. crude valuations. But the mechanism — Hormuz-driven export slump, not demand destruction — means the build is a symptom of the crisis, not evidence the crisis is easing. You cannot use a Hormuz-caused domestic build to argue that oil is heading back to $70. The geopolitics creating the build are the same geopolitics justifying the price.

My read on today: The Houthi attack on the Tihamah in Bab el-Mandeb is the most important new variable, and the market has not yet fully priced it. Saudi Arabia's alternative export route to Yanbu depends on the Red Sea being accessible. If the Houthis sustain and extend their Bab el-Mandeb blockade — as they have now signaled with their first fatal strike in over a year — there is no geographic escape valve for Gulf producers. The dual-chokepoint scenario is no longer a tail risk; it is a live threat. The desk is not bearish on oil today, even with a historic inventory build, because the Houthi escalation has cancelled out whatever diplomatic optimism the Oman talks were generating.

The two-sided picture: The bearish case is an imminent Oman-Iran deal formalizing 5–8M bpd of partial Hormuz access — combined with the massive EIA build reminding the market that U.S. domestic supply is not as tight as a six-session rally implied. That combination could take Brent back toward $83–$85 quickly. The bullish case is a sustained Houthi closure of Bab el-Mandeb that forecloses the Yanbu alternative — in which case, even a Hormuz partial deal doesn't solve the supply problem, and $90–$95 comes back into view. The Tihamah attack has materially shifted the probability distribution toward that latter scenario.

The call: Brent holds $85–$89 today, with $87 as the near-term anchor. I am not chasing this down, because the dual-chokepoint risk is real and likely underpriced. A Hormuz partial-deal announcement is the sharpest single downside catalyst; a confirmed Houthi escalation in Bab el-Mandeb is the sharpest upside catalyst. Until one of those resolves, this is a range-bound market — not a trending market — and should be traded as such.
