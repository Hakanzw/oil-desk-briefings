💯 Century Mark Holds: Brent Consolidates Above $100 as Triple-Corridor Supply Crisis Continues

Brent Holds $100 After Week's 13%+ Surge — Houthi Kinetic Embargo, Kazakhstan CPC Export Halt and Hormuz Impairment Deliver Simultaneous Disruptions to Three Critical Supply Corridors With No Modern Precedent

Brent crude held above the $100-per-barrel threshold on Friday, consolidating at approximately $100.30 after surging past the century mark on Thursday — the first breach since late May — as markets absorbed the simultaneous disruption of three of the world's most critical oil export corridors. The week's 13%+ gain for Brent, the largest in months, was built on compounding supply-architecture failures: Yemen's Houthis executed the first kinetic enforcement of their Saudi maritime embargo by striking oil tankers Encelia and Layla with ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and drones; drone attacks knocked the Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Black Sea terminal offline and forced Tengiz output cuts at Kazakhstan's largest oilfield, disrupting approximately 1.6 million barrels per day; and the Strait of Hormuz continued to operate at severely impaired capacity after Secretary of State Rubio declared the Hormuz ceasefire MOU 'effectively dead.' No modern market precedent exists for a simultaneous shutdown of this many major export arteries.

The Kazakhstan dimension of this crisis arrived with less fanfare than the Houthi strikes but is structurally equally significant. Drone attacks attributed to Ukraine struck tankers Asia and Nissos IOS at the CPC Novorossiysk terminal on July 17–20, forcing a halt to exports from a pipeline system that processes approximately 1.6 mb/d — roughly 76% of Kazakhstan's total production. The bulk of that output flows from the Tengiz field, a Chevron-operated super-giant. Kazakhstan implemented unplanned production cuts to manage the inventory buildup; the CPC terminal's infrastructure was reported intact and ready to resume once security conditions allow, but no timeline has been confirmed. For European buyers who had increasingly relied on CPC crude as a non-Gulf alternative since the Hormuz impairment began, the disruption removes one of the last meaningful substitution pathways available to them.

Friday's session is functioning as a consolidation and positioning reset after five consecutive sessions of gains. Trump's threats of 'major military punishment' for further Houthi attacks — including reported consideration of a 'massive attack' on Iran — maintain a strong asymmetric risk premium anchored in the tape. Technically, Brent's close above $100 on Thursday was the cleanest confirmation that the century mark now functions as a floor rather than a ceiling, pending a credible de-escalation signal on at least one of the three disrupted corridors. No such signal is visible entering the weekend. With the OPEC+ ministerial on August 2 now the next major scheduled catalyst, markets will trade headline risk at current levels or modestly higher through Friday's close.

Red Sea / Houthis

Houthis Strike Saudi Tankers Encelia and Layla With Ballistic Missiles and Drones — Embargo Now in Kinetic Phase

Yemen's Houthi rebels struck two Saudi-linked oil tankers in the Red Sea on Thursday — Encelia and Layla — using ballistic missiles, cruise missiles, and drones, marking the first kinetic enforcement of the maritime blockade declared July 20. Both vessels were located approximately 70 nautical miles southwest of Al Shuqaiq when fires broke out aboard. Houthi spokesman Yahya Sarea said the tankers 'violated the embargo imposed by the group's armed forces' against vessels calling at Saudi ports. At least nine tankers had already altered their Red Sea routes following Monday's blockade declaration; Thursday's strikes will dramatically extend that number. The Bab el-Mandeb Strait — through which approximately 12% of global trade passes — is now under active military enforcement. Cape of Good Hope rerouting adds 2–3 weeks to Asia-Europe voyages and directly compresses Saudi Arabia's effective export capacity to Asian buyers.

Kazakhstan / CPC

Kazakhstan Cuts Tengiz Output After Drone Attacks Force CPC Black Sea Terminal Offline — 1.6 mb/d Disrupted

Kazakhstan implemented unplanned production cuts at its Tengiz oilfield — a Chevron-operated super-giant and the country's largest — after drone attacks struck tankers Asia and Nissos IOS at the Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Novorossiysk terminal on July 17–20, forcing an export suspension. The disruption threatens approximately 1.6 million barrels per day, representing roughly 76% of Kazakhstan's total production. Russia and Kazakhstan have attributed the attacks to Ukraine. CPC infrastructure was reported intact and ready to resume but no security timeline has been established. The halt removes one of the last credible non-Gulf crude substitution pathways for European buyers — buyers who had already been paying a premium to source away from the Hormuz-impaired Gulf since the conflict began.

OPEC+ / Output

OPEC+ Approved Fifth Consecutive Monthly Hike of 188k bpd for August — Paper Quotas Cannot Reach Impaired Shipping Lanes

Seven OPEC+ members — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — agreed on July 6 to raise collective output by 188,000 barrels per day starting in August, the fifth consecutive monthly increase continuing the unwinding of 2023-era production cuts. Analysts have called the paper quotas 'essentially meaningless' in the near term given that physical flows remain constrained by Hormuz impairment and the newly enforced Red Sea embargo. Kazakhstan — one of the seven hike signatories — has since implemented emergency output cuts at Tengiz due to the CPC disruption, further undermining the coalition's ability to translate agreed volumes into market barrels. The August 2 ministerial will be a decisive test of whether OPEC+ pivots to a defensive price-floor posture as Saudi tanker exports remain under active Houthi missile attack.

Demand / IEA

IEA: Global Oil Demand Set for First Annual Decline Since 2020 — US-Iran War and Hormuz Impairment Destroying Economic Activity

The International Energy Agency's July Oil Market Report projected global oil demand will fall by approximately 1 million barrels per day in 2026 — the first annual contraction since the COVID-19 pandemic. The forecast attributes the demand collapse to economic activity destruction caused by the US-Israel-Iran conflict and the sustained impairment of the Strait of Hormuz, which handles roughly 20% of seaborne oil. The report underscores the structural tension at the heart of the current market: supply is disrupted enough to push Brent to $100, but the economic damage from $100 oil is simultaneously destroying the demand base that would otherwise support higher prices. The IEA also projected Henry Hub natural gas to average $3.57/MMBtu in Q4 2026, up sharply from Friday's ~$2.92 spot level as LNG route anxiety builds with the Red Sea entering active combat.

The Guru's Verdict — Friday, July 24, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Yesterday's briefing called $100 Brent a question of timing, not direction, absent a credible diplomatic reversal. The century mark arrived as forecast. Thursday's surge to approximately $100.69 — the first settlement above $100 since late May — confirmed what the compounding disruption architecture had been signaling all week: the market was not waiting for a new catalyst but simply catching up to a supply reality five consecutive sessions in the making. The century mark is no longer resistance. It is the new floor.

Friday's modest pullback is exactly the consolidation a $13 week deserves. Nothing in the geopolitical or supply picture has changed: the Houthi maritime embargo is in active kinetic enforcement after striking two Saudi tankers with ballistic missiles; the CPC Black Sea terminal remains offline with 1.6 mb/d of Kazakhstan's exports disrupted; the Strait of Hormuz continues to operate at severely impaired capacity; and Trump is publicly considering a 'massive attack' on Iran. Three supply corridors are simultaneously broken in a configuration without modern precedent, and the diplomatic architecture that might restore any one of them does not exist.

The IEA's 1 mb/d demand decline forecast for 2026 is the structural complication that keeps a simple bullish call from being a home run. At $100 Brent, demand destruction becomes a real, measurable force rather than a theoretical projection. Industrial buyers are already pricing the switch from oil to alternative fuels into Q3 procurement decisions. The question is whether the demand side can destroy enough barrels to offset a supply architecture that has lost Hormuz, Red Sea, and CPC simultaneously — and my answer, given the depth and breadth of the disruptions, is not in the near term. The supply disruptions are structural; the demand response will build more slowly.

My call for today: Brent trades $97–$103, biased flat-to-higher into weekend positioning. The week is closing; major participants will not add significant length into a two-day gap risk window. But the absence of any de-escalation signal — on Houthis, CPC, Hormuz, or US-Iran — means the floor defense is real and the bid is present. A weekend headline (a Houthi strike on a third tanker, a Trump infrastructure bombing announcement, or a CPC timeline extension) is the primary upside catalyst. A surprise US-Iran diplomatic contact that reopens the Hormuz MOU track is the only meaningful downside catalyst, and nothing in Rubio's record over the past 72 hours suggests that is coming. The $100 floor holds unless someone blinks. Nobody is blinking.
