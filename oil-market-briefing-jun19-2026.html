⚡ The Clock Starts: MOU Signed at Bürgenstock — Hormuz Normalization Now Official

US and Iran Execute Peace Deal at Swiss Ceremony — Brent Slides to a 4-Month Low as the 30-Day Hormuz Clock Starts and First Tankers Exit the Gulf

The moment markets have priced for five days finally arrived. US and Iranian officials signed the 14-point Memorandum of Understanding at the Bürgenstock resort near Lucerne on Friday, formally starting a 30-day window to complete mine-clearing in the Strait of Hormuz and restore full commercial transit. Brent fell 2.7% to ~$77.22 per barrel — its lowest close since late February and down roughly 9% for the week, the steepest weekly drop since the deal announcement. WTI slipped 2.1% to ~$74.94, breaking the $75 support level that had held through the prior three sessions. Ships carrying stranded crude began making their way out of the Persian Gulf before the ink had dried, and Kuwait said publicly that it will start ramping production back up — a first tangible signal from the physical supply side.

The ceremony answered the first-order question and immediately raised the second. Before and during the Bürgenstock proceedings, Iran's newly established Persian Gulf Strait Authority (PGSA) continued to collect transit fees reported at up to $2 million per passage, settled in Chinese yuan. The US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control had already issued a formal May 1 alert warning that payments to Iranian-linked entities for Hormuz passage "could constitute prohibited transactions" under US sanctions law, exposing non-US firms to secondary sanctions. The MOU's text does not appear to explicitly dissolve the PGSA or waive OFAC restrictions on fee payments, leaving shippers in a legal grey zone: the deal is signed, but the sanctions maze surrounding it is not yet cleared. Maritime underwriters have not formally downgraded their Hormuz war-risk ratings — the functional prerequisite before most tanker operators will commit to transit.

Beyond the legal fog, the physical constraints governing a Hormuz return have not shifted. Mine clearance carries the most conservative official timeline, with Congressional assessments pointing to up to six months for a full clearance of Iranian-laid ordnance. Roughly 500 commercial vessels remain trapped in the Persian Gulf and cannot exit simultaneously through the 21-mile-wide navigable channel; barnacle encrustation and onboard maintenance backlogs add weeks of preparation time before ocean-going voyages are feasible. Production field restarts range from days for Saudi Arabia's pipeline-accessible volumes to months for Iraq, which depleted in-country storage during the closure. Kuwait Petroleum Corporation's managing director told the S&P Global Energy conference this month that KPC targets 70% of its 2.6 mb/d output capacity within 6–8 weeks of a confirmed reopening, with full recovery in 10–12. The signature in Bern starts the clock. The oil does not flow on cue.

Today's Key Headlines

01
Ceremony / Bern

US–Iran MOU Signed at Bürgenstock — Formal 30-Day Hormuz Normalization Clock Running

US and Iranian officials signed the 14-point Memorandum of Understanding at the Bürgenstock resort in Switzerland on Friday morning, converting the June 14 deal announcement into a binding treaty framework. The MOU's key provisions: Iran commits to full commercial transit through Hormuz within 30 days, immediate oil-sales rights for Tehran, and the lifting of US oil sanctions in exchange for Iran's nuclear-weapon forswear pledge. The signing was conducted at a location selected jointly by Pakistan and Qatar — the two principal mediators — with UN observers present. Brent fell 2.7% to its lowest close since late February on the event. Ships carrying stranded crude had already begun exiting the Gulf before the signing concluded, suggesting the physical market had been pricing the outcome for days.

Implementation Lag

Even With a Signed Deal, Full Oil Flow Could Take Weeks or Months — Mines, Ships, Fields

PBS NewsHour reports that a full resumption of Hormuz oil flows faces structural obstacles that cannot be dissolved by a ceremony: mine clearance estimated at up to six months, approximately 500 commercial vessels trapped in the Persian Gulf that cannot exit simultaneously through the 21-mile-wide navigable channel, barnacle-encrusted vessels requiring weeks of maintenance before ocean transits are feasible, and production field restarts ranging from days (Saudi Arabia) to months (Iraq, which depleted in-country storage). Energy flows are projected to reach 80% of prewar levels by September at the earliest. Iran's potential demand for transit tolls adds legal complexity on top of the logistical backlog — ship captains and maritime insurers may take their time committing to passage.

Iran Tolls / OFAC

Iran Collects Up to $2M-per-Transit Fees in Yuan — OFAC Warns Shippers of Sanctions Exposure

The signing did not resolve a festering complication: Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority continues to charge transit fees reported as high as $2 million per passage, paid in Chinese yuan — the first new toll imposed on an international strait in more than 160 years. The US Treasury's OFAC issued a May 1 alert stating that payments to Iranian-linked entities for Hormuz passage could constitute prohibited transactions, exposing non-US firms to secondary sanctions. The MOU's language does not clearly dissolve the PGSA or provide a payments safe harbor. Marine underwriters and ship captains are waiting for explicit US Treasury guidance before downgrading Hormuz war-risk ratings and committing to transit — a bottleneck that could meaningfully slow the practical reopening pace even within the formal 30-day window.

Kuwait / Production

KPC: Kuwait Targets 70% of 2.6 mb/d Output in 6–8 Weeks, Full Recovery in 10–12

Kuwait Petroleum Corporation's managing director Shaikh Khaled Ahmad Al-Sabah told the S&P Global Energy Middle East conference on June 3 that KPC targets recovery of approximately 70% of Kuwait's 2.6 million barrels per day capacity within six to eight weeks of a confirmed Hormuz reopening, with the remaining 30% requiring roughly one additional month. KPC's refinery operations — approximately 1.4 mb/d of capacity — could return to normal in two to three weeks. Kuwait had been producing 2.8 mb/d before February 28; no tankers have loaded at Kuwaiti export terminals since the conflict began. KPC's 10–12-week full-recovery timeline is among the most optimistic in the Gulf, suggesting that the fastest route back to material new supply runs through Kuwaiti terminals once safe passage is formally confirmed by maritime insurers.

Russia / Sanctions

US Lets Russian Oil Sanctions Waiver Expire June 17 — Third Lapse, Now With Iran Supply Returning

The White House allowed the temporary waiver of Russian oil sanctions to expire for the third consecutive time on June 17 — but for the first time without an immediate renewal. At the G7 summit in France, Trump said Washington is now "in a position" to enforce sanctions on Russian crude because Iranian oil is returning through Hormuz. Russia had exported an average of 6 million barrels per day of crude in May. Indian and Chinese buyers who have relied on Russian supply throughout the Hormuz disruption now face a sharply altered landscape: tighter OFAC enforcement on Russian crude arriving simultaneously with the opening of Iranian supply channels. Whether Asian refinery demand rotates cleanly from Russian to Iranian barrels — or both face enforcement headwinds simultaneously — is the supply-competition story markets will spend H2 2026 tracking.

Market Outlook

Goldman, Morgan Stanley See Brent at $80 in Q4 — Citi Projects $70; IEA Warns of 2027 Structural Oversupply

With the deal signed, major banks have revised oil price targets. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley both project Brent at approximately $80 per barrel in Q4 2026, reflecting gradual supply normalization offset by recovering demand. Citi is the most bearish, projecting $70 per barrel on a scenario of faster-than-expected Hormuz clearance and Iraqi production ramp. The IEA has issued a longer-term structural warning: if post-war investment flows resume and Iranian production recovers at full pace, global oil supply could increase by 8 million barrels per day by 2027 against demand growth of only 2 mb/d — a structural oversupply scenario that the current forward curve does not reflect and that the July 5 OPEC+ ministerial will be the first opportunity to formally confront.

The Guru's Verdict — Friday, June 19, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Yesterday I argued that the Switzerland ceremony was the clearest binary event on the oil calendar this year — and that Tehran's economic incentive to finalize the deal after four months of Hormuz-closure-induced fiscal stress made a clean signing the base case. The binary resolved in the bearish direction. The MOU is signed, Bürgenstock's proceedings are on the record, and for the first time since February 28 the Strait of Hormuz has a formal reopening obligation attached to a running countdown clock. Brent is at $77.22 — down 9% this week — its lowest since late February. The war premium is not just absent; the market is beginning to price supply that has not yet been produced, shipped, or cleared through a channel that still has mines in the water.

My read on today: The signing clears the first hurdle but raises three immediate questions that will determine price direction over the next 30 days. First: does the PGSA toll controversy get resolved via US Treasury guidance, or does it freeze ship captains in a legal grey zone? Maritime insurers have not yet formally downgraded Hormuz war-risk ratings — that step, not the ceremony, is the practical green light for most tanker operators, and it has not happened yet. Second: how fast does the Gulf producer ramp actually track? Kuwait's 6–8 week timeline and Iraq's months-long restart are very different price paths. Third: with the Russian sanctions waiver expired for the first time without a renewal, does tighter OFAC enforcement on Russian crude meaningfully offset some of the Iranian supply returning to Asian buyers — or do both channels face headwinds simultaneously? That supply-supply competition has not yet been priced.

The bigger picture: The deal is signed but the plumbing isn't fixed. The gap between a signed MOU and the first full tanker convoy through a certified-clear Strait remains days-to-weeks at minimum, weeks-to-months at maximum. With commercial inventories 7%+ below the five-year average, the SPR at a 43-year trough, and approximately 500 ships navigating a simultaneous exit, the downside from here is real but not unlimited. Citi's $70 target requires faster-than-expected implementation across every variable. Goldman's $80 target assumes a normal pace. The IEA's 2027 oversupply warning — 8 mb/d of new supply against 2 mb/d of demand growth — is the structural bear case the market will start pricing after it sees the first convoy through a cleared Strait, not before.

My call: Brent holds $73–$78 today, consolidating the signing-day loss as markets shift focus to implementation metrics and the PGSA toll question. The direction is clearly lower — but not linearly so. A single OFAC enforcement action against a toll-paying tanker, an Iranian patrol-vessel confrontation at the Strait exit, or any indication the 30-day window is slipping would snap crude back $5–7 in a session from current levels. The war is over on paper. The oil market is about to find out what that means in practice.
