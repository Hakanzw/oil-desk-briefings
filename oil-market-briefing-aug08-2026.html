Week in Review — The Corridor Window Opened and Slammed Shut in Five Sessions

Brent's Wild Week: $88 → $79 → $83.55 as Iran's Exclusionary Corridor Terms and Thursday Strikes Erase the Deal Discount — Weekend Diplomacy Is the Sole Variable for Monday's Open

Markets are closed Saturday, but the oil desk is not. This week was among the most volatile five-session stretches of the 2026 crisis — a week that opened with Brent near $88, saw it crash toward $79 on the strongest corridor deal optimism since June, then snapped back to $83.55 by Friday's close as Iran struck "hostile targets" in the Strait of Hormuz near Qeshm Island and proposed corridor terms that would ban U.S. and Israeli vessels from the waterway. The round-trip of nearly $9 per barrel unfolded in five sessions. The corridor discount that markets priced in from Monday through Wednesday evening has been fully erased. Weekly performance: Brent down approximately 5% from last Friday's $87.93 close; WTI down approximately 8% from $84.67.

The anatomy of the week is instructive for understanding what remains unresolved. Monday opened with Brent gapping lower as President Trump signaled renewed diplomatic engagement following appeals from Gulf allies, and crude fell further as optimism mounted toward a corridor signing. By mid-week, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC "there is a chance we may have a deal" within hours, and Secretary of State Marco Rubio described negotiations as being in their "final gate" — acknowledging "progress made, but not finality." Oil fell below $80 as the market priced a signing as imminent. Kyle Rodda of capital.com captured the broader dynamic: "Fridays are for fighting but Mondays are for the markets in Trump's world, with crude prices gapping lower at the open." Then Thursday's session inverted everything: Iran struck what it described as "hostile targets" near Qeshm Island, Brent surged approximately 4.9%, and by Friday the emerging Iran-Oman framework was revealed to include a demand to exclude U.S.- and Israeli-flagged vessels — a term Washington has explicitly ruled out as incompatible with freedom-of-navigation principles.

The OPEC+ backdrop completed the week's picture. On August 2, seven core members — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — approved a final 188,000 barrels per day quota increase for September, completing the formal rollback of the voluntary cuts announced in April 2023 and restoring OPEC+ to pre-April-2023 quota levels on paper. The group's official statement retained "full flexibility to increase, pause or reverse" future changes. Rystad Energy's Head of Geopolitical Analysis Jorge Leon noted that "OPEC+ has little incentive to rush into further supply changes" and flagged a Q4 production pause as the most likely next step. The EIA's inventory report for the week ending July 31 — released August 5 — confirmed a 2.48 million barrel crude build concentrated at Cushing, Oklahoma, alongside a more constructive 3.47 million barrel distillate draw. The fundamental read is mixed; the market has set it aside entirely in favour of the diplomatic binary.

Geopolitical — Lead

Iran Proposes 'Middle Corridor' Excluding U.S. and Israeli Vessels — Rubio: 'Progress Made, But Not Finality'; Deal Window Closes Without Signing

Iran and Oman are negotiating a 'middle corridor' through the Strait of Hormuz: a single two-way route with inbound traffic via a northern lane through Iranian waters and outbound via a southern lane through Omani waters, initially as a 60-day interim arrangement with no transit fees during that period. However, Iran's framework reportedly includes a ban on vessels flagged or owned by the United States or Israel, and Iran has floated longer-term compensation levies on nations it classifies as hostile. Qatar, Pakistan, and Oman are coordinating to exchange drafts between Washington and Tehran. Secretary of State Marco Rubio acknowledged 'progress made, but not finality,' while Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC 'there is a chance we may have a deal' within hours — those hours passed without a signing. Iran then struck 'hostile targets' near Qeshm Island on Thursday, and Friday's corridor term disclosures confirmed that the gap between Iranian demands and US freedom-of-navigation principles has widened, not narrowed, since Tuesday's optimism peak.

03
OPEC+ / Supply

OPEC+ Completes Rollback of 2023 Cuts With Final 188,000 bpd September Quota — Rystad Energy Sees Q4 Pause

Seven OPEC+ members — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — approved a 188,000 barrel per day production quota increase for September 2026 at their August 2 virtual meeting, formally completing the phased restoration of the 1.65 million bpd removed from markets starting April 2023. The group stated it would 'closely monitor and assess market conditions' and retained 'full flexibility to increase, pause or reverse' future adjustments. A separate 2 million bpd in cuts dating to 2022 remain active through the end of 2026. Rystad Energy's Jorge Leon said 'OPEC+ has little incentive to rush into further supply changes' and anticipates a formal Q4 pause at the September 6 JMMC meeting. Analysts broadly describe September's quota as disconnected from deliverable reality: with Hormuz shipping still at roughly 15% of its pre-war baseline, paper increases cannot translate to additional delivered barrels without a functional corridor.

EIA / Inventories

EIA Week Jul 31: +2.48MB Crude Build to 407.0M Barrels — Cushing +2.36MB; Distillates Draw 3.47MB; SPR Falls to 304.8MB

The EIA's Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week ending July 31 showed US commercial crude oil inventories rising 2.48 million barrels to 407.0 million barrels — a larger-than-consensus build that would normally pressure WTI. The increase was concentrated at the Cushing, Oklahoma WTI delivery hub (+2.36 million barrels to 21.0 million), which nevertheless remains 9.47 million barrels below the five-year seasonal average. Total commercial crude stocks sit 6% below the five-year norm. Distillate fuel inventories (diesel and heating oil) drew 3.47 million barrels, placing stocks 14.5 million barrels below the five-year median heading into heating season — a signal of sustained downstream demand. Gasoline stocks drew 1.64 million barrels. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve fell another 2.84 million barrels to 304.8 million, continuing a multi-month depletion. The next EIA report — for the week ending August 7 — releases August 12 and will be the first post-Thursday-escalation inventory read.

Hormuz / Shipping

Hormuz at ~15% of Pre-War Baseline — Gulf Crude Exports Climbed to 4.8 mb/d in July From May's Crisis Low of 1.6 mb/d

Despite the diplomatic impasse, Gulf crude exports via Hormuz have climbed from a May crisis low of 1.6 million barrels per day to approximately 4.8 million barrels per day in July — a partial recovery enabled by US military escort operations and sporadic convoy arrangements, but still far below the pre-war norm of roughly 17–18 million bpd. Visible ship traffic tracked by AIS shows some vessels transiting with transponders switched off. The International Maritime Organization has reported approximately 20,000 stranded mariners and 2,000 ships in the Persian Gulf as of April's crisis peak. Iran's Thursday strikes on 'hostile targets' near Qeshm Island, combined with the parallel emergence of its exclusionary corridor proposal, have cast doubt on near-term recovery. The Houthis struck the Saudi-affiliated tanker Wafa with ballistic missiles in the Red Sea on August 5 — the eighth Saudi vessel struck since Houthis announced a Bab el Mandeb pressure campaign in late July — compounding the Persian Gulf constraint with a parallel Red Sea threat.

Natural Gas

Henry Hub ~$2.68 — Well Below EIA's Q3 Forecast of $3.37/MMBtu; Hormuz LNG Constraint a Structural Support Floor

Henry Hub natural gas spot prices are tracking near $2.68 per MMBtu — materially below the EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook Q3 2026 forecast of $3.37/MMBtu and well beneath the $3.50/MMBtu annual average the agency projected in January 2026. The most recent confirmed YCharts spot price (August 3) registered $2.81/MMBtu, up 8.49% from July 31's $2.59, attributed to heat-driven cooling demand. Henry Hub's domestic character has insulated it from the full crude oil geopolitical risk premium, but the Hormuz LNG disruption provides a structural underpinning: before the crisis, approximately 20% of global LNG transited the strait, and European buyers who rerouted to alternative suppliers absorbed higher delivered costs, indirectly supporting US LNG export margins. If the Damietta LNG terminal in Egypt — struck by drone on July 29 — sustains lasting damage, that bypass narrows further and could push Henry Hub above $3.00/MMBtu.

The Guru's Verdict — Saturday, August 8, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

This week was a masterclass in how fast the corridor trade unwinds — and how asymmetrically the market prices diplomatic hope versus Iranian escalation. Five sessions, nearly $9 of intra-week movement on Brent, and the benchmark ended approximately 5% below where it started. The narrative has shifted fundamentally, even if the price has not fully reflected it. The week opened with markets pricing an imminent corridor signing. It closes with markets repricing a structural impasse. Iran's proposed exclusionary vessel terms are not a negotiating footnote — they are a statement of political intent. The demand to ban US and Israeli vessels is not a term Washington can accept under any diplomatic framework compatible with its longstanding positions on maritime law. The gap has widened, not narrowed.

My weekend read: Oman's mediation remains the most plausible off-ramp, and Iran has economic incentives to reopen the strait that ultimately outweigh its political maximalism on vessel exclusions. But the timeline has shifted from hours to weeks. The market is appropriately repricing that extension at Friday's close of $83.55. The correct framework for Monday's open: a geopolitical risk premium of $5–7 above the fundamental clearing price. With the fundamental picture pointing toward a supply glut if the corridor opens — as Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and the IEA all confirm — the underlying clearing price without the risk premium sits in the mid-to-high $70s. Brent at $83.55 reflects a risk premium that is elevated but not stretched. It prices a corridor delay, not a corridor collapse.

OPEC+ context: The September quota completion matters less than most headlines suggest. Seven nations formally restored their pre-April-2023 quota levels on paper on August 2 — but those barrels cannot reach most buyers while Hormuz handles 15% of its normal traffic. OPEC+'s paper production ceiling and its physical delivery capacity are two entirely different numbers, and Rystad's Q4 pause call is the only rational response to that disconnect. The group will not accelerate quotas into a corridor it does not control. Watch September 6 for the formal confirmation.

My call for the coming week: Brent $80–$88, with the center of gravity near $83–$85 absent fresh escalation. Downside requires a weekend diplomatic breakthrough that modifies Iran's exclusionary terms — achievable but not signaled. Upside requires a hardened Iranian position confirmed by Washington — probable if weekend contacts produce no progress and Iran reaffirms Thursday's military posture. The asymmetry: in this tape, headline risk is skewed upward through the weekend because Iranian escalation generates immediate market reaction while diplomatic progress generates a slower grind lower. Do not chase upside above $88 without confirmed military escalation. Do not short below $81 without corridor language that Washington explicitly accepts as compatible with freedom-of-navigation principles.
