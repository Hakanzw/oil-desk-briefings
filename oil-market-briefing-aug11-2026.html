Hormuz Stalemate — Crude Consolidates After Monday's 5% Surge on "Only Semi-Negotiating" Comment

Brent Holds Near $88 as Trump Admits U.S. Is "Only Semi-Negotiating" With Iran — Hormuz Risk Premium Locked In at a New High

Brent crude is consolidating near $87.78/bbl Tuesday — pinned at its highest level since late March — after Monday's dramatic 5% single-session surge repriced the entire Hormuz resolution timeline. The catalyst was President Trump's comment to Axios on Sunday that the United States is "only semi-negotiating" with Iran and plans to rely on its naval blockade rather than further airstrikes to pressure Tehran. Markets interpreted the remark as a direct admission that a near-term deal is off the table, adding roughly $4/bbl to Monday's open and sending WTI back above $82 for the first time since early August.

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi is holding a firm line. His conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz — an end to the U.S. naval blockade, eased sanctions, and war reparations — remain publicly unchanged, and Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei underscored Monday that the blockade must be lifted as a precondition to any agreement. With only 8–15 vessels now transiting daily versus the pre-conflict average of 130, the strait's closure is costing global markets roughly 17 million barrels per day in normal throughput. Analyst Tim Waterer of KCM Trade described the market mood: the lack of concrete movement, together with lingering questions about any agreement's practical details, is keeping a meaningful risk premium embedded in every Brent contract.

Tuesday's session is relatively quiet on scheduled data, leaving headline risk as the dominant price driver. The EIA's weekly petroleum status report due Wednesday will be the next hard datapoint — the first print since the surprise 2.479-million-barrel crude build for the week ending July 31. Meanwhile, a separate Houthi drone strike on the Saudi Aramco refinery in Jizan on Saturday, swiftly extinguished with no reported casualties, is a reminder that the infrastructure risk premium is not confined to the strait: Saudi export assets are also in the crosshairs. The desk watches for any overnight Oman channel signal that Washington is prepared to offer a substantive concession on the blockade — that remains the sole near-term downside catalyst.

Houthis Strike Aramco Refinery in Jizan — Fire Quickly Extinguished, No Casualties Reported

Houthi forces launched a drone strike on the Saudi Aramco refinery complex in Jizan on Saturday, the southwestern Saudi city near the Yemeni border. Turkey and Iraq Ink One-Year Kirkuk-Ceyhan Pipeline Deal at 750,000 bpd — Baghdad's Only Viable Export Route

Ankara and Baghdad on August 1 signed a one-year transit agreement keeping crude flowing through the Kirkuk-to-Ceyhan Oil Pipeline at 750,000 bpd — against a theoretical maximum of 1.5 million bpd. The deal is effectively Iraq's only functioning export corridor to the Mediterranean, as the Hormuz closure has collapsed Baghdad's oil revenues from roughly $6 billion per month to under $2 billion. Both governments are negotiating a longer-term framework spanning oil, electricity, and water cooperation.

Egypt and Libya Near $1 Billion Tobruk-to-Alexandria Pipeline Deal to Bypass Gulf Disruption

Cairo and Tripoli are finalizing a joint feasibility study for an 800-km oil pipeline connecting Libya's Tobruk terminal to Egypt's Alexandria port — estimated to cost over $1 billion. The project would give Egypt direct access to Libyan crude to replace suspended Gulf supplies, with Cairo seeking at least one million barrels per month. Libya's production has meanwhile climbed to decade-highs, giving Tripoli a compelling export incentive. Both sides are actively evaluating financing structures and an implementation timeline.

The Guru's Verdict — Tuesday, August 11, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Trump's "only semi-negotiating" comment is doing more work this Tuesday than any scheduled data release. Strip away the noise and the signal is straightforward: the U.S. president has publicly demoted the Hormuz talks from active deal-making to something short of that, in the same week that Iran's Foreign Minister codified his three non-negotiable conditions. The gap between Washington and Tehran is not narrowing on any publicly visible metric. That gap is what Brent's $87–$88 handle is pricing.

My read on today: Monday's 5% surge was the market's rational response to a shift in the negotiation posture — not a speculative overshoot. Brent at $87.78 is orderly. The move from $84 to $88 in a single session reflects a genuine repricing of the resolution timeline, not a panic: the prior $84 level had already embedded some optimism about Oman-brokered progress, and Trump's candor erased it. Today's quiet +0.07% consolidation is the market digesting that repricing.

The two-sided picture: The bearish scenario requires the Oman channel to deliver a meaningful concession — most plausibly a U.S. softening on the naval blockade, perhaps styled as a "confidence-building" measure — within the next session or two. That is not impossible; back-channel deals have surprised before. The bullish tail is the Iran parliament scheduling a vote on the ship-ban bill, a Houthi strike that actually knocks out Saudi production, or Trump hardening further ("not negotiating at all"). Any of those puts $93+ back on the board. China's demand destruction — crude imports at a 10-year low — remains the one structural buffer preventing a full return to the March shock levels.

The call: Brent holds $86–$90 today, with the midpoint around $87–$88 as the base case absent a headline. I am not chasing yesterday's surge, but I am not fading it either — Iranian conditions must credibly narrow and Washington must visibly soften before the risk premium justifies unwinding. Watch the Oman channel and Wednesday's EIA inventory print. The next meaningful directional move is, again, headline-driven.
