⚡ Day Two — The Premium Keeps Bleeding Out: Text Agreed, Signing Friday

Crude Holds Two-Month Lows as US–Iran Deal Heads to Friday Signing and the Hormuz Reopening Clock Starts

Crude steadied near its lowest levels since early spring on Tuesday, a day after the announcement that the United States and Iran had agreed the final text of a deal to end the war — with an official signing ceremony confirmed for Friday, June 19, in Switzerland. Brent sat around $83.82 after sliding roughly 4% in the prior session, while WTI held near $80.23, down about 5.5% to a two-month low. President Trump authorized the toll-free reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and the immediate removal of the US naval blockade of Iran's ports; a US official said the strait would stay open toll-free for 60 days under the pact.

The implementation clock is now the story. Washington says traffic through Hormuz could return to normal "within 30 days" once Iran commits to removing the mines it laid — and the US says it knows where every one of those mines sits. At the G7 summit, leaders took up the demining question directly, with Britain and France signaling they would help clear the strait once the conflict formally ends. The diplomacy is converging; the physical reopening is the variable the tape is now trading.

Yet the market is not pricing a clean, instant return of barrels. Two of the thorniest issues — Iran's nuclear program and the long-standing US sanctions regime — went unaddressed in the weekend announcements, leaving room for friction before pens hit paper Friday. With Gulf fields shut for months, infrastructure damaged, and the spare-capacity buffer thinned by the UAE's exit from OPEC+, traders are repricing the probability of supply returning faster than its actual arrival.

US Crude Stocks Keep Drawing — Down 7.2M Barrels in the Latest Week

US commercial crude inventories fell 7.2 million barrels in the week ending June 5, with refinery runs rising to 17.0 million bpd at 95.3% of capacity, per the EIA. The prior week (ending May 29) saw an 8.0-million-barrel draw to 433.7 million barrels — roughly 3% below the five-year average. The string of draws underscores that physical balances remain tight even as the geopolitical premium deflates; Wednesday's report (week ending June 12) is the first inventory print into the post-deal tape.

Thin Cushion: Symbolic OPEC+ Hike and the UAE Exit Leave Little Margin

The Guru's Verdict — Tuesday, June 16, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Yesterday I wrote that the war premium was gone and that what remained was an implementation trade. Day two has not changed that thesis — it has sharpened it. The text is agreed, the signing is on the calendar for Friday in Switzerland, the blockade is lifted, and crude is sitting on two-month lows: Brent near $83.82, WTI near $80. The market is no longer pricing whether a deal happens; it is pricing how fast steel actually moves through Hormuz.

My read on today: The G7 demining conversation is the most important tell on the screen. If Britain and France formally join the clearance effort and Iran starts pulling mines, the US "within 30 days" line becomes credible and the glide toward the high $70s resumes. But you cannot un-mine a strait with a communiqué — and the fact that the nuclear file and sanctions went unmentioned in the weekend announcements is the crack through which a relief rally could still come.

The bigger picture: The risk profile stays genuinely two-sided, and the cushion is thin. With the UAE out of OPEC+ and spare capacity depleted, the market has no shock absorber if Friday's signing wobbles on wording. The bearish base case — clean ceremony, visible demining — points lower. The bullish tail — a dispute over the nuclear language or a no-show — would snap Brent back toward $90+ in a single session.

My call: Brent holds $80–$88 today, biased lower into the Friday signing but with violent upside if the framework cracks. I remain neutral-to-bearish outright and would still rather own optionality than direction here. The premium is gone; the only question left is the speed of the barrels — and that is a logistics timeline, not a diplomatic one.
