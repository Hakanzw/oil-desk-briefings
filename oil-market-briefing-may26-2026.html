Top Story

⚡ Breaking — Peace Deal "Largely Negotiated," But US Strikes Resume

Trump: Iran Deal "Will Be Announced Shortly" — US Conducts Self-Defense Strikes Hours Later as Rubio Flags Wording Disputes

The oil market is navigating its most whipsawed week since the conflict began. On Saturday, Trump announced a peace deal with Iran was "largely negotiated" and would be announced shortly, calling Gulf leaders from the Oval Office and declaring the framework included a memorandum of understanding. Oil plunged over 10% across the week on the news. Then, Monday morning: US Central Command announced it had conducted "self-defense strikes" in southern Iran, targeting missile launch sites and boats allegedly attempting to emplace mines around the Strait of Hormuz — just hours after Iran's delegation met Qatari mediators in Doha.

Secretary of State Rubio told reporters Tuesday the deal is being held up by disputes over the precise wording — particularly around Iran's nuclear programme and sanctions relief — estimating resolution was "a couple of days" away. Iranian semi-official media, meanwhile, said disputes over "one or two" issues were jeopardizing the deal, and accused the US of "obstructionism" over frozen Iranian funds. Iran has not officially commented on the US strikes, but state-linked outlets framed them as ceasefire violations.

Brent, which had sunk toward $92–$95 on deal optimism, rebounded to ~$98 Tuesday morning as traders repriced the strike risk. WTI diverged sharply — down 5% — reflecting rising US domestic inventories and its relative insulation from the Hormuz premium.

Diplomacy

Trump Says Deal "Largely Negotiated" — Rubio Says Wording Disputes Could Take "A Couple of Days"

On Saturday, Trump posted that a peace agreement with Iran — including a memorandum of understanding as a first phase, followed by broader nuclear talks within 30–60 days — was "largely negotiated" and would be announced shortly. He said he personally called the leaders of Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Pakistan, Turkey, Egypt, Jordan and Bahrain from the Oval Office. By Tuesday, Rubio was telling reporters on his plane to India that the deal remained stalled over specific wording — particularly on Iran's nuclear programme, the lifting of sanctions, and the release of frozen Iranian funds. Tehran's semi-official outlets said "one or two" issues threatened to unravel the framework, and accused Washington of obstructionism on the funds question.

Military

US "Self-Defense Strikes" Hit Iranian Missile Sites and Mine-Laying Boats Hours After Doha Talks

US Central Command announced early Tuesday that it had conducted strikes in southern Iran targeting missile launch sites and boats suspected of attempting to deploy mines around the Strait of Hormuz — framing the action as self-defense to "protect our troops from threats posed by Iranian forces." The strikes came just hours after Iran's delegation, led by parliament speaker Qalibaf, had met Qatari mediators in Doha for war talks. Iranian state-linked media called the strikes ceasefire violations. Tehran had not officially commented by press time. Markets repriced higher on the news: Brent bounced ~2% to ~$98 from its post-deal-optimism lows near $92.

03
Abraham Accords

Trump Demands Muslim Mediators Join Abraham Accords — Pakistan Resists, Saudi Position Unchanged

In a complication that nearly derailed mediation over the weekend, Trump posted that all Muslim countries involved in the Iran peace process should "mandatorily" join the Abraham Accords as a condition. Pakistan — which has never recognised Israel and whose Army Chief Munir has been central to the mediation — roundly rejected linking the two issues, with a source telling Reuters they were "not interlinked and cannot be made so." Saudi Arabia's position on the accords also remains unchanged, a Saudi source told CNN. Trump later softened somewhat, acknowledging one or two countries could opt out, in what analysts read as a tacit carve-out for Pakistan.

04
Market Reaction

Brent–WTI Spread Blows Out — Divergence Signals Domestic vs. Global Supply Dynamics

One of the clearest market signals this week is the extreme Brent–WTI spread. While Brent has partially rebound to ~$98 after the US strikes, WTI dropped about 5% to ~$91.73 — the two benchmarks telling very different stories. Analysts at eToro explained the divergence: "Brent prices Middle Eastern crude more directly — any threat to Hormuz shipping pushes it up. WTI is more a reflection of domestic US supply, and inventories have been building for a few weeks now." UBS warned Friday that despite the week's selloff, global oil market strain remains acute: observed inventories dropped 246 million barrels in March and April, and cumulative production losses could exceed 1 billion barrels by end of May.

Nuclear Sticking Point

Iran's Supreme Leader: Enriched Uranium Must Stay in Iran — "No Dust, No Dollars" Says Trump

A fundamental impasse threatens to sink the deal framework: Iran's Supreme Leader has reportedly insisted that enriched uranium must remain on Iranian soil, citing vulnerability to future US and Israeli strikes if the material were sent abroad. Trump responded with characteristic bluntness — saying Iran's uranium would either be transported to the US, dealt with on-site, or taken to another location: "No dust, no dollars." The nuclear question is the deepest structural disagreement between the two sides, and one Rubio's "couple of days" timeline may be optimistic about. Qatar's mediating team remains in Tehran through the week.

No WTI Settlement Monday on US Holiday — Japanese Oil Tanker Arrives Home After Hormuz Transit

Markets were partially closed Monday for US Memorial Day, with no WTI settlement, contributing to Tuesday's exaggerated percentage moves. Separately, a notable milestone: the first Japanese oil tanker to transit the Strait of Hormuz during the conflict arrived at Chita City on Honshu after successfully transiting the strait in April. Chief Cabinet Secretary Kihara called it "pleasant news in terms of securing stable energy supply." Japan has released a historic volume of strategic petroleum reserves to cushion the crisis. The ship's arrival, 12+ weeks after the war began, underscores just how protracted even the first trickle of restored supply flows has been.

The Guru's Verdict — May 26, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Twelve weeks into this crisis and the market is doing something it hasn't done since the war began: it's trying to price a deal. Brent is down over 10% in a week. That is an extraordinary move — and it tells you just how much war premium was baked in at $107. The question now isn't whether a deal happens. It's whether this week's fresh US strikes — targeting Iranian missile sites and mine-laying boats just hours after Tehran's delegation sat down in Doha — have poisoned the well.

My read on today: The market's 2% Brent bounce this morning on the strikes is a gut reaction, not a structural repricing. What matters is Iran's official response. If Tehran issues a formal ceasefire violation statement, expect a $5–8 Brent spike. If they say nothing — or even criticise the strikes while confirming continued engagement — the deal remains alive and Brent drifts back toward $94–$96. I lean toward the latter. Iran is closer to a deal than at any point in this conflict. They would not let a targeted military action in the strait — framed as defensive by Washington — become the reason to walk away from a potential end to a war they are losing.

The bigger picture: Rubio's "couple of days" timeline is the most significant thing said in energy markets this week. The nuclear enrichment question — Iran insists the uranium stays, Trump says "No dust, no dollars" — is the only true deal-breaker left. Everything else is wording. Third-country custody arrangements, phased sanctions relief, Abraham Accords linkage — these are solvable problems. If that one nuclear question finds a formulation both sides can live with, this deal closes this week and Brent goes to $85.

My call: Brent holds $93–$103 today, with the range entirely determined by Tehran's response to the strikes and any leak from the Doha talks. I am no longer calling this a bullish market. The supply fundamentals remain severe — UBS is right that cumulative losses could hit 1 billion barrels by month's end — but the deal risk is real, it is imminent, and for the first time since February, the price direction of least resistance is down.
