📉 Century Mark Surrendered: Brent Falls 3.9% Late Friday on Pakistan-China Diplomatic Signal — But Triple-Corridor Disruption Intact

Brent Retreats From $100 to $96.78 as Pakistan and China Push for New US-Iran Talks — Saudi Tanker Blockade, CPC Halt and Hormuz Impairment Remain Simultaneously Active as Market Pivots to Weekend Diplomatic Watch

Brent crude surrendered the $100 century mark in a sharp late-session reversal on Friday, closing at $96.78 — down 3.88% — after reports broke that Pakistan, with Chinese diplomatic backing, was pushing for a new round of US-Iran negotiations. WTI settled at $89.31, down 3.12%. The move is significant in isolation but must be read in the context of the week's full arc: Brent entered Monday near $88 and exits Friday's close near $97 — a net weekly gain of roughly 9% — meaning the reversal retraced only a fraction of one of the largest weekly crude rallies in months. The diplomatic signal that caused Friday's selloff has not translated into a ceasefire, a weapons pause, or any restart of shipping through the three disrupted corridors. It has given traders a reason to reduce long exposure over the weekend gap — no more, no less.

The structural disruption picture that drove Brent above $100 on Thursday remains fully intact entering the weekend. Yemen's Houthi movement is enforcing a maritime blockade against Saudi-linked tankers in the Red Sea through kinetic military means: Saudi crude loadings through the Bab el-Mandeb have fallen 36% from their July peak as tanker operators divert to the Cape of Good Hope route, adding two to three weeks to Asia-bound voyages. Simultaneously, the Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Black Sea terminal at Novorossiysk remains offline following drone attacks on tankers Asia and Nissos IOS in mid-July, with Kazakhstan's 1.6 million barrels per day of Tengiz exports still suspended with no restoration timeline. The Strait of Hormuz continues to operate at severely impaired capacity with Secretary Rubio having declared the ceasefire MOU "effectively dead." No credible de-escalation pathway exists on any of the three disrupted corridors as of Friday's close.

The Pakistan-China diplomatic move is the first credible external mediation signal since the Hormuz MOU breakdown. Markets are giving it partial weight — a 4% crude sell-off rather than the 8–10% that a confirmed ceasefire framework would likely deliver. The structure of the trade for early next week is binary: either diplomatic contacts develop into a framework over the weekend and crude retraces further toward the low $90s, or the weekend passes without progress and Brent gaps back toward — and through — $100 on Monday's open. The OPEC+ ministerial on August 2 — now eight days away — adds another layer of complexity: the coalition must decide whether to proceed with a sixth consecutive 188,000-bpd paper increase or pivot to a defensive price-floor posture while Saudi crude exports remain under active Houthi attack.

Pakistan and China Push for New US-Iran Talks — Brent Surrenders $100 in Late-Friday Reversal

The diplomatic signal was the first credible external mediation overture since Secretary Rubio declared the Hormuz MOU 'effectively dead' days earlier. Red Sea / Houthis

Saudi Crude Loadings Via Red Sea Collapse 36% as Houthi Kinetic Blockade Enters Second Week

Saudi crude exports through Bab el-Mandeb — which had been tracking near record levels in early July — have fallen 36% as tanker operators avoid the Red Sea under the Houthi military blockade declared July 20. The Houthis followed their declaration with kinetic enforcement: ballistic missiles, cruise missiles and drones struck Saudi-linked tankers Encelia and Layla, setting one ablaze. Shipping analysts estimate that sustained enforcement of the blockade could remove 7% from effective global oil supply as Cape of Good Hope rerouting adds weeks to transit times for Asian buyers. Saudi Arabia's Yanbu port — through which approximately 4 million barrels per day of crude transits to Asia — is the focal point of the disruption, and the blockade has no articulated exit condition Saudi Arabia can satisfy unilaterally.

Supply / India

India Turns to Venezuela and Angola as Hormuz Crisis Cuts Middle East Crude Access — Venezuela Now Third-Largest Supplier

Indian state refiners — cut off from traditional Middle East term supplies by the Strait of Hormuz impairment — are aggressively sourcing replacement crude from Venezuela and Angola. Venezuela has become India's third-largest crude supplier, with BPCL and IOC importing an estimated 12.51 million barrels of Venezuelan crude in a single month — the highest volume since February 2020. India is also testing new Angolan grades not previously processed at its refineries. The supply scramble reflects a broader structural rerouting of global crude flows as the Hormuz crisis enters its fifth month, with Asian buyers who historically depended on Gulf crude now competing for Atlantic Basin barrels against European refiners already displaced from Russian supply.

Saudi Arabia

Saudi Red Sea Crude Exports Hit Record High Before Houthi Blockade Declaration Reversed Flows in Days

Saudi Arabia had ramped crude exports from its Red Sea ports to a record high in early July as the kingdom attempted to route volumes around the Hormuz impairment and maintain market share with Asian buyers. The record was short-lived: the Houthi maritime blockade declaration on July 20 and subsequent kinetic enforcement forced a rapid and dramatic reversal. Shipping transponder data shows tankers going dark — with transponders switched off — as vessels near the Bab el-Mandeb assess risk in real time. Analysts note the reversal represents a double-bind for Saudi Arabia: its Gulf export route is compromised by Hormuz, and its Red Sea route is now under active military attack.

Russia / Black Sea

Ukraine Strikes Extend to 150+ Black Sea Vessels — CPC Terminal Offline; Kazakh Exports Suspended With No Timeline

Ukraine has struck more than 150 Russian tankers and associated vessels in the Black Sea and Sea of Azov as part of its campaign to degrade Russia's shadow fleet and export infrastructure. The Caspian Pipeline Consortium's Novorossiysk terminal — struck in mid-July — remains offline with no confirmed resumption date, keeping Kazakhstan's 1.6 million-barrel-per-day Tengiz output suspended. The dual disruption — Red Sea under Houthi blockade and Black Sea under active Ukrainian targeting — removes both of the major crude export routes that European buyers developed as Hormuz alternatives since the US-Israel-Iran war began in February.

EIA Reports Surprise 2.0 mb Crude Build — But Stocks Remain 6% Below Five-Year Average as Refinery Runs Hit 96.1%

The Energy Information Administration's weekly petroleum status report for the week ending July 17 showed a 2.0-million-barrel crude build — well above the 1.1-million-barrel draw that analysts had forecast — bringing total commercial crude stocks to 411.7 million barrels, still 6% below the five-year seasonal average. Gasoline stocks added 0.8 million barrels and distillate stocks built 1.4 million barrels. Refinery utilization reached 96.1% of operable capacity as processors ran at maximum throughput ahead of peak summer demand. The July 17 data predates the worst of the Houthi kinetic enforcement; next week's report covering the July 25 week will be the first full reading to reflect the new disruption regime across all three corridors.

The Guru's Verdict — Saturday, July 25, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Friday's briefing held that the $100 floor was real unless someone blinked, and that nothing in Rubio's record suggested a diplomatic contact was coming. The Pakistan-China initiative is the blink — partial, unconfirmed, and fragile, but real enough to send Brent down nearly 4% in the final hours of the trading week. This is not a reversal; it is a repositioning. Traders who were long crude into a two-day gap window cut exposure on the first credible external mediation signal in weeks. That is rational risk management, not a change in the underlying supply equation. Brent ends the week at $96.78 — up more than 9% from Monday's open — and every barrel of structural disruption that drove that gain is still missing from the market.

The supply architecture that drove Brent above $100 on Thursday has not changed by one barrel entering the weekend. The Houthi maritime blockade is in kinetic enforcement with Saudi crude loadings down 36%. The CPC Black Sea terminal remains offline with 1.6 million barrels per day of Kazakh exports suspended. The Strait of Hormuz continues to operate at severely impaired capacity, and the MOU that was supposed to govern it has been declared effectively dead by the US Secretary of State. Three of the world's most critical oil export corridors are simultaneously disrupted, and the diplomatic signal that moved markets Friday has not repaired a single one of them. India is importing Venezuelan crude. Saudi tankers are going dark in the Red Sea. European refiners have lost their last non-Gulf, non-Russian substitute. The physical reality of this market is $95+ Brent until something structural changes.

The Pakistan-China initiative deserves a measured assessment rather than dismissal. Pakistan has historically served as a back-channel between Washington and Tehran, and China's backing gives it economic weight. But the preconditions for US engagement are steep: Iran would need to credibly commit to Hormuz reopening before any administration could justify a ceasefire framework without that provision. Tehran's posture has hardened since Rubio's MOU declaration. The probability that the weekend produces a formal framework that moves crude materially lower on Monday's open is real but remains a tail scenario, not the base case. The base case is that the Houthi blockade, CPC suspension, and Hormuz impairment remain fully intact on Monday morning.

My call for next week's open: Brent trades $93–$100, with Monday's direction entirely driven by whether weekend diplomatic contacts produce a concrete statement. Base case — silence or vague encouragement — means Brent gaps up 1–2% on Monday as Friday's position-cutting reverses and the underlying triple-corridor scarcity reasserts itself. Bull scenario for crude: confirmed Iranian engagement with the Pakistan-China framework; Brent extends toward $91–$93. Bear scenario for crude bulls: Trump authorizes strikes on Iranian civilian infrastructure over the weekend; Brent opens above $106. The century mark is not far. Unless diplomacy firms into something concrete this weekend, we go back there before month-end.
