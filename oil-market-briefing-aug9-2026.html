⚡ Developing — Hormuz Deal 'In Principle' as Saudi Arabia Is Caught in a Pincer

Iran and Oman Agree 'In Principle' on New Strait of Hormuz Shipping Routes — But Tehran Says Full Opening Requires US Military Concessions

Iran and Oman announced on Thursday that they have reached an in-principle agreement on the coordinates of new commercial shipping routes through the Strait of Hormuz, with a joint statement described as being in the "final drafting stage." Under the proposed arrangement, commercial vessels would enter the Persian Gulf through an Iranian-controlled corridor and exit through a lane overseen by Oman — a compromise structure designed to allow trade to resume without Tehran formally ceding control of the strait it has blockaded since February. President Trump, calling progress "very good," said the Strait would reopen "soon," and a US official told reporters an announcement could come as early as Wednesday.

But Iran has drawn a sharp and public line: Tehran's Foreign Ministry explicitly stated the Oman arrangement cannot be equated with a full reopening, and that restoring unrestricted passage requires the United States to remove its naval presence and halt strikes on Iranian infrastructure. That gap — between Trump's "soon" and Iran's stated conditions — is the single most important variable in oil markets this week. The 50/50 probability estimated by one senior Gulf official familiar with the talks reflects how finely balanced the outcome remains.

Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia faces a structural threat that has transformed the supply calculus entirely. When Iran shut the Strait of Hormuz in late February, Riyadh pivoted rapidly to its East-West Pipeline, pumping up to ~4 million barrels per day through the Red Sea terminal at Yanbu — a fourfold increase over pre-conflict levels. Since late July, Houthi forces have declared a maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia and struck at least eight Saudi tankers in the northern Red Sea, vowing to close every exit point. If both Hormuz and Yanbu are simultaneously disrupted, the kingdom — the world's largest oil exporter — would face a supply-side crisis without a near-term relief valve.

Saudi Arabia / Houthis

Houthis Declare Red Sea Blockade — Saudi Arabia's Only Oil Escape Route Under Fire

Yemen's Houthi movement declared a maritime blockade of Saudi Arabia on July 21–22 and has since struck at least eight Saudi oil tankers in the northern Red Sea, including the Wafa off Yanbu on August 5. Saudi Arabia had rerouted ~4 million barrels per day of crude through Yanbu after Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz — a fourfold increase over pre-conflict levels. Houthi forces are now targeting this escape valve. At least seven tankers diverted or paused voyages after the initial warning, and Brent surged briefly above $95/bbl when the blockade was announced before retracing.

OPEC+

OPEC+ Completes Voluntary Cut Rollback — 188,000 bpd September Hike Closes Book on 2023 Cuts; Q4 Pause Signaled

On August 2, seven core OPEC+ countries — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — approved a 188,000 bpd output increase for September, completing the full rollback of the 1.65 million bpd voluntary supply cut first agreed in 2023. Sources signaled the group will then pause further increases in Q4, with Saudi Arabia believed to be working to defend a price floor in the low $80s. Analyst Jorge Leon at Rystad said the group "has little incentive to rush into further supply changes." A deeper layer of roughly 2 million bpd in baseline cuts from 2022 remains in place through year-end.

EIA Inventory

US Crude Inventories Post Surprise +2.479 Million Barrel Build — Bearish Counterpoint to Supply-Risk Story

The latest EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report showed US commercial crude inventories rising by 2.479 million barrels for the week ending approximately July 31, against a consensus forecast for a 1.5 million barrel draw. Inventories now stand in the 404–410 million barrel range, roughly 7% below the five-year seasonal average. The build came despite elevated refinery runs (averaging 17.3 mb/d) as domestic crude supply increased. The unexpected result adds a meaningful bearish undertone to a market otherwise dominated by geopolitical risk premium.

US natural gas futures continued their slide into August, with Henry Hub dropping from $3.22/MMBtu on July 1 to $2.66 on August 7 — a decline of roughly 17% in five weeks. The Guru's Verdict — Sunday, August 9, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Saudi Arabia is caught in a pincer. Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz in February forced the kingdom to pivot its export infrastructure entirely to Yanbu on the Red Sea, running that terminal to near-maximum capacity at ~4 mb/d. The Houthis identified this dependency and moved — and now the escape hatch is under fire. The kingdom that was supposed to be OPEC+'s swing-capacity anchor is itself squeezed between two blockades it cannot resolve unilaterally.

My read on today: The Iran-Oman 'in principle' Hormuz agreement is real and represents genuine diplomatic progress — but it is carefully circumscribed. Iran has not surrendered control of the strait; it has agreed to a commercial corridor mechanism while preserving its political leverage against the US. Whether that mechanism translates into meaningful tanker flows in the coming days is a different question from whether the diplomatic framework exists. This week's announcement window is the market's most important scheduled event since the June signing ceremony that failed to deliver.

The bearish case builds: A formal Hormuz announcement this week — combined with the OPEC+ September hike completing the voluntary cut rollback and a bearish EIA/IEA double on Tuesday — could push Brent below $80, perhaps to the high $70s. The physical pipeline is ready: Iranian, Qatari, and Gulf crude have been waiting since February. The bullish tail is real: If Houthi strikes succeed in materially disrupting Saudi Yanbu flows, the market confronts a supply shock that no OPEC+ hike or diplomatic communiqué can quickly replace. In that scenario, $95/bbl returns fast.

My call: Brent trades in a $79–$88 range this week, with dominant sensitivity to the deal announcement timeline. The base case is a partial, caveated Hormuz reopening that eases — but does not eliminate — the geopolitical premium. The extreme risk scenario — both Hormuz and Yanbu simultaneously disrupted — is not the base case, but it is live, and the market has not fully priced it.
