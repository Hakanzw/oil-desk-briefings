Breaking — The Second Peace Pivot: Trump Calls Off Iran Strike, Crude Plunges 5–7%

Trump Cancels Planned 'Massive Attack' on Iran — Oil Slides 5–7% as Diplomatic Talks Set to Resume Monday

Two days after Iran's IRGC struck two US-escorted tankers in the Strait of Hormuz to close out July, President Trump made a stunning reversal: aboard Air Force One on Sunday night, he announced he had ordered US forces to stand down from what he called a planned "massive attack" on Iranian infrastructure. Trump said allies in the Middle East — including Saudi Arabia — had urged a diplomatic path instead. New talks are expected to begin Monday. The market delivered its instant verdict: Brent crude for October fell as much as 7.3% intraday to $81.55 before recovering toward $83.87, while WTI touched $79.62 before steadying near $80. The session opened with one of the sharpest single-day drops since June's peace deal, when a framework agreement briefly pushed crude to a two-month low.

The context for Trump's pivot is the extraordinary arithmetic of July. In just 31 days, Brent rose approximately 21% — the strongest monthly advance since early 2022 — driven entirely by Hormuz supply removal: tanker throughput collapsed to roughly 11–14 vessels per day against a pre-war baseline of 88–100. Brent briefly touched $100 per barrel on July 23 following reports of Iranian strikes on Kuwaiti infrastructure. By late July, Tehran had signalled willingness to halt attacks if the United States reciprocated; Friday's IRGC tanker strike had appeared to extinguish that signal — until Trump's Sunday statement reopened it. The swing from July 31's $87.93 close to Monday's intraday low of $81.55 is a $6.38 two-day round trip and captures exactly the geopolitical volatility that has defined 2026 crude.

Market participants are pricing cautious optimism, not euphoria. The June 15 framework — which saw Brent drop into the low $80s only for implementation failures to push prices back toward $90 — taught traders that diplomatic announcements and physical oil delivery are two different things. The Strait of Hormuz still contains Iranian-laid mines whose clearance Congress was told could take up to six months. Gulf production fields shut in during the conflict must be restarted. And the IRGC's July 31 tanker strike is only two days old. The war premium is deflating again, but the supply physics have not changed overnight.

Supply Corridor

Turkey and Iraq Sign One-Year Kirkuk-Ceyhan Pipeline Deal — 750,000 bpd Minimum as Hormuz Alternative

Turkish state firm BOTAS and Iraqi state companies SOMO and NOC signed a one-year agreement on August 1 to maintain flow through the 986-kilometre Kirkuk-Ceyhan pipeline at a minimum guaranteed capacity of 750,000 barrels per day. The deal has become strategically critical because Iran's Strait of Hormuz closure slashed Iraq's monthly oil revenues from approximately $6 billion to under $2 billion — making the Mediterranean export route through Turkey a lifeline for Baghdad's budget. The agreement represents one of the few Hormuz-bypass corridors that has actually expanded during the conflict, providing a measure of supply-chain resilience for a producer otherwise trapped behind an Iranian military chokepoint.

European Energy

Hungary's Paks Nuclear Plant Faces Full Shutdown After Danube Hits Record Low — First in 44 Years

Hungary's Paks nuclear plant — which provides nearly half the country's electricity — is bracing for a complete shutdown after the Danube River fell to record low levels and disabled cooling pumps across all four reactor blocks. Prime Minister Péter Magyar warned of an impending national energy crisis, and neighbouring countries are assessing reserve capacity availability. The episode underscores Europe's compounding energy vulnerability: with Middle East supply disruptions already stressing global crude and gas markets, the loss of a major nuclear baseload source would force Hungary toward heavier fossil-fuel imports at elevated prices. The Paks situation is the most significant European energy infrastructure threat since the 2022 gas crisis.

The Guru's Verdict — Monday, August 3, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Oil markets opened Monday with one of the most disorienting price sessions of a year that has been nothing but disorienting price sessions. Two days after the IRGC struck US-escorted tankers in the Strait of Hormuz — cementing July's 21% run as the strongest monthly advance since early 2022 — President Trump announced he had called off a planned massive attack on Iran. Brent fell 7.3% intraday to $81.55 before recovering toward $83.87. WTI briefly broke below $80. The war premium, which it took all of July to build, gave back nearly a third of itself in a single overnight session. That is the geometry of a geopolitical-risk trade in a market where the asset and the geopolitics move faster than the underlying supply physics.

My read on today: This is the same trade as June 15, but with the market older and wiser. In June, Brent fell to the low $80s on Trump's 'deal complete' declaration, then spent six weeks clawing back toward $90 as implementation failed and the IRGC resumed attacks. The setup today is structurally identical: a diplomatic announcement, no mine-clearing, no field restarts, no change to the 14-transits-per-day Hormuz reality — just a statement. What's different is that both sides have since demonstrated a willingness to use military force within days of diplomatic overtures, which means the credibility window is shorter and the scepticism discount is already priced in. The selloff is real and the diplomacy may be real — but the floor is also real. Stocks at 404.5 million barrels, 6% below the seasonal average, do not lie.

The bigger picture: Big Oil's record Q2 earnings season — Exxon at $14.5 billion, Chevron at a company-record $12.1 billion, Shell at its best quarter in four years — provides the structural anchor. These numbers reflect Q2 averages when WTI was running at $92.45. If Trump's diplomatic pivot collapses the war premium to the mid-$70s, the earnings picture for Q3 changes materially, and the Permian Basin's role as the critical swing producer reasserts itself. But if diplomacy again proves to be an announcement without implementation — as June demonstrated — the Q3 print will look a great deal like Q2. The oil market is in a credibility-testing mode, and the test runs on weeks, not hours.

My call: Brent $79–$88 today, with the bias firmly toward the lower half on initial diplomacy optimism — but with the tail risk emphatically to the upside. Any IRGC action, Iranian official pushback on the talks, or breakdown in the US-Iran contact today sends Brent back above $90 before the New York close. Respect the diplomatic signal but do not front-run the physics. The Strait of Hormuz is still mined, the fields are still shut, and the history of this conflict says never to price the press release as the policy.
