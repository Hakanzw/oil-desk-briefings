Top Story

⚡ Price Milestone: WTI Breaks Below $70 — Brent Nears Pre-War Close — Qatar Ras Laffan Explosion Keeps Natural Gas Risk Alive

WTI Crude Falls Below $70 for the First Time Since the February Conflict Began — Brent at $72.63 Has Erased ~50% of the War Premium — Qatar LNG Disaster and Unresolved IAEA Dispute Complicate the Bearish Narrative

Thursday delivered the price milestone oil traders have been watching since the June 17 MOU: West Texas Intermediate crude slipped below $70 per barrel for the first time since the conflict began on February 28, settling around $69.47 (−1.25%) as Brent fell to $72.63 (−1.51%) — a level that has erased approximately 50% of the war premium that drove North Sea Dated crude to $144 in April. The reversion is extraordinary in pace and completeness. Six weeks after the first headlines of a framework deal and eight days after the June 17 MOU was signed, the crude market has essentially concluded that the peace architecture is durable enough to price supply normalisation — even as the IAEA inspection dispute festers and Bürgenstock implementation talks remain without a rescheduled date. Against crude's decline, natural gas moved sharply higher, rising 0.96% to $3.25/MMBtu — a divergence driven by the ongoing assessment of an explosion and fire at Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex on June 21–22 that killed at least 13 workers and injured 66, casting uncertainty over the pace of LNG supply normalization precisely when European importers are pricing a clean post-war restart. Hormuz transit data released this week offered the most concrete supply-return evidence yet: 93 vessels transited the Strait in the June 19–21 weekend alone versus 32 the prior weekend, with estimated flows reaching 12 million barrels per day — still below pre-war levels but moving rapidly in that direction.

The Qatar Ras Laffan disaster is the most significant fresh supply-side surprise since the June 17 MOU, and it sits entirely outside the Iran–US diplomatic track. The facility — Qatar's primary LNG export hub and one of the world's largest, handling the majority of Qatar's 77 million tonnes per year of export capacity — was in the process of restarting operations after sustaining damage from Iranian missile strikes during the conflict. The June 21–22 explosion during that restart killed 13 workers and injured 66; Qatar's Energy Ministry stated that export capabilities remain unaffected, a claim markets are treating with cautious skepticism given that complex restarts are inherently prone to secondary failures. Dutch TTF natural gas futures spiked on news of the explosion; Henry Hub followed with the +0.96% gain visible in today's session. The broader implication runs deeper than a single session move: even as crude's diplomatic-driven decline accelerates, the LNG recovery track — which underpins European energy security assumptions for winter 2026 — carries independent operational risk that has not been fully priced. Natural gas rising as oil falls is not a decorative divergence. It is a structural signal that the energy transition from war-disruption to normalcy is uneven and fragile.

The diplomatic picture has evolved without resolving. UN nuclear chief Rafael Grossi said Wednesday that IAEA inspectors "will" visit Iran's nuclear facilities — a statement that, for the first time, introduced IAEA institutional authority into a dispute that had previously been a US–Iran public messaging fight. But Iran's position remains that nuclear inspections occur after a final deal, not during the current 60-day negotiation window. The gap between Grossi's statement and Tehran's framing may, paradoxically, be a narrowing: if both sides can characterise inspections as a post-deal deliverable while engineering a 'technical contact' visit in the near term, the public dispute could be managed without either side explicitly conceding. The Bürgenstock cancellation meanwhile has no confirmed rescheduling date. Yet the market is voting with its feet: the fact that Brent continued to fall through all of this week's diplomatic noise is the clearest signal yet that traders regard the commercial architecture — above all, the Treasury's 60-day general license expiring August 21 — as more load-bearing than the inspection timetable. Russia's deepening fuel crisis adds a distinct layer: the Kapotnya refinery, Moscow's primary regional fuel supplier, is now confirmed by Reuters as offline until at least 2027 following repeated Ukrainian drone strikes, and more than 20% of Russia's total refining capacity has been destroyed — a structural constraint on the global refined-products market that persists regardless of what happens in Hormuz.

Markets / Price Milestone

WTI Breaks Below $70 — Brent at $72.63 Nears Pre-War Close as Five-Week Peace Discount Fully Prices In

West Texas Intermediate crude fell below $70 per barrel for the first time since the conflict began February 28, settling around $69.47 (−1.25% on the day; intraday low $69.08); Brent followed at $72.63 (−1.51%). From Brent's April wartime peak of ~$144, the decline now reaches approximately 50% — achieved in roughly six weeks of post-framework repricing. The Brent–WTI spread narrowed to approximately $3.16, from $3.84 on Wednesday, as WTI led the selloff in the US session. The move came without a discrete new catalyst: Hormuz transits continue to rise, the Treasury's 60-day general license remains in force, and the market is mechanically pricing supply return. The WTI $70 break is the most-watched round-number level of the post-war trade; its breach sets the stage for a test of the OPEC+ fiscal breakeven floor in the mid-to-upper $60s.

Russia / Refining

Moscow's Kapotnya Refinery Offline Until 2027 — Over 20% of Russian Refining Capacity Destroyed

The Kapotnya oil refinery — Moscow's primary regional fuel supplier, responsible for more than a third of the capital region's refined fuel — was struck twice this month by Ukrainian drones and will not resume production until at least 2027, according to a Reuters report Wednesday. More than 20% of Russia's total refining capacity has been knocked offline across the conflict; over two-thirds of Russia's 83 regions face fuel rationing or supply disruptions. Russian petroleum output runs approximately 12% below last year's levels. Deputy Prime Minister Novak has signaled the government is considering a complete diesel export ban. The Kapotnya development reinforces the IEA's finding that global refinery throughput will fall roughly 2 mb/d to 82 mb/d in 2026 — a structural constraint on how quickly falling crude prices translate into lower consumer fuel costs.

Hormuz / Shipping

93 Vessels Through Hormuz in One Weekend — Flows Hit 12 mb/d; 11,000 Seafarers Still Stranded

Tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz surged dramatically over the June 19–21 weekend, with 93 vessels transiting versus just 32 the prior weekend (June 12–14); at least 39 additional transits were recorded on Monday, June 22. Estimated flows reached 12 million barrels per day — up from a May low of 9.6 mb/d but still well below pre-war levels of 20+ mb/d. The Joint Maritime Information Center lowered its regional maritime threat level to "moderate" on June 18. However, Iranian mines remain in the Strait; the US mine-clearance operation is ongoing and Congress was previously told full clearance could take up to six months. The IMO is simultaneously coordinating the evacuation of more than 11,000 stranded seafarers through Hormuz in cooperation with Iran, Oman, and the US.

UN Nuclear Chief Grossi Says IAEA Inspectors 'Will' Visit Iran — Tehran Says Only After a Final Deal

UN nuclear chief Rafael Grossi said Wednesday that IAEA inspectors "will" visit Iran's nuclear facilities — the first public IAEA statement consistent with VP Vance's June 23 claim that Iran had agreed to inspections. However, Iran's position remains that inspections occur only after a final deal is concluded, not during the current 60-day MOU window. Grossi's formulation may offer a face-saving path: if both sides can characterise inspections as a post-deal deliverable while engineering a preliminary 'technical contact' in the near term, the public dispute could be managed without concessions. NPR's reporting frames the episode as a genuine gap in what the June 17 MOU actually commits each side to — a gap that the Bürgenstock implementation session, now canceled, was specifically designed to close.

China / Demand

China Teapot Refiners at Lowest Throughput Since 2017 — IEA Sees Global Demand Down 1.1 mb/d Year-on-Year

China's independent ('teapot') refineries have cut operations to their lowest levels since 2017, reflecting months of reduced crude availability during the Hormuz crisis and ongoing economic softness. The IEA's June 2026 Oil Market Report quantified the broader demand impact: global oil demand is down 1.1 mb/d year-over-year in 2026, with Q2 deliveries approximately 5 mb/d below the same period last year. OECD government stockpiles dropped 163 million barrels to their lowest level since December 1990. The IEA projects global supply at 102.4 mb/d in 2026 — down 13.6 mb/d from pre-conflict levels. The teapot cutbacks are a critical near-term demand signal: as Iranian crude flows return and Gulf producers ramp, Chinese import appetite will determine whether supply return translates into sustained inventory builds or is absorbed by recovering demand.

The Guru's Verdict — Thursday, June 25, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Thursday's session delivered the number the market has been counting down toward since June 17: WTI below $70, Brent at $72.63, and the war premium that dominated this market for four months now approximately 50% erased. The symmetry is almost too clean — Brent at $144 in April, $72 in June — but the underlying dynamics are more complex than a round-number narrative suggests. The market is not simply unwinding a risk premium. It is also pricing a demand contraction. The IEA says global oil demand is running 1.1 mb/d below year-ago levels; China's teapot refiners at 2017-low throughput rates represent a demand-side drag that compounds the supply-return signal. The bullish tail is alive but requires a catalyst: the IAEA inspection dispute, Qatar Ras Laffan's uncertain restart, Russian diesel ban risk, and the ever-present mine risk in Hormuz all represent genuine upside scenarios that are not priced at current levels. Brent at $72.63 is not a risk-free environment. It is an environment in which the market has decided the dominant force is supply return — and is treating everything else as temporary noise.

My read on today: The market is telling us something important: even IAEA controversy, a Bürgenstock cancellation, and a deadly explosion at Qatar's LNG hub were not enough to arrest the crude decline. That is a bearish signal in its purest form — bad news doesn't move price anymore. When negative headlines fail to produce rallies in a declining market, it means the supply-return story has enough momentum that risk events are treated as temporary diversions rather than structural threats. The IAEA dispute is real and the Bürgenstock cancellation is genuinely unsettling — but the Treasury's 60-day general license through August 21 provides a commercial floor that does not depend on nuclear inspection timing. That commercial architecture, not diplomatic goodwill, is what the market is pricing. The war premium is gone. What's pricing now is the pace of the supply return.

The bigger picture: Russia's worsening fuel crisis and Qatar's LNG explosion are the two stories most likely to produce non-linear outcomes in energy markets over the next 30 days. A Russian full diesel export ban would be structurally bearish for refined-product crack spreads globally — particularly for European and Asian importers who have been routing around Russian fuel for months. Qatar's Ras Laffan, if damage is more extensive than the Energy Ministry claims, could spike European gas prices significantly ahead of winter storage-filling season. Neither event would reverse crude's trajectory, but both could produce sharp moves in adjacent energy markets that matter for consumer energy costs and for the broader IEA supply-recovery scenario. The war premium in crude is gone. The war's structural damage to global energy infrastructure is not.

My call: Brent consolidates in the $70–$75 range over the next several sessions, with the lower bound increasingly set by the OPEC+ fiscal breakeven logic — at $70 Brent, multiple Gulf producers operate below breakeven, and Saudi Arabia has both the incentive and the latent production capacity to stabilise. The natural gas trade is arguably more interesting: the Qatar risk premium may have further to run if Ras Laffan damage assessments disappoint, and European winter storage season begins in earnest next month. Oil's war premium is history. The implementation trade — mines, restarts, diplomatic plumbing — is what remains, and natural gas is where the next sharp move may come from.
