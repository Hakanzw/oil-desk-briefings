⚡ Month-End Pullback — Brent Retreats 2% From Near-$90 High as Hormuz Inches Toward Recovery and Damietta Widens the Energy War

Brent Slides to $85 on Final Day of July as Hormuz Shows First Shipping Signs of Life — Drone Strike on Egypt's Damietta LNG Hub Opens Dangerous New Front in Middle East Energy War

July 31 arrived with oil markets stepping back from the precipice of $90. Brent crude slid $1.76 to $85.10, while WTI fell $1.91 to $81.70, as a confluence of month-end profit-taking, dollar strength, and the first tentative signs of Hormuz transit recovery cooled the supply-shock rally that had defined the past week. Both benchmarks still close out July with gains of roughly 19–20% — one of the strongest monthly performances of the year — as the Strait of Hormuz crisis, the Abqaiq shutdown, and the broader US-Iran war continue to underpin the structural price floor. The retreat is orderly; it is not a trend break. Brent remains roughly $10 above its pre-Abqaiq-strike level, and every key supply constraint that drove the rally remains intact heading into August.

The session's most significant new development came not from the Strait of Hormuz but from the Mediterranean. On July 29, a drone of unattributed origin struck two LNG-related vessels at Egypt's Damietta port — the US-owned Energos Winter and the Gaslog Salem — triggering fires that burned into the night before Egyptian emergency services contained them. Neither Iran nor Yemen's Houthi rebel movement claimed responsibility, and Egyptian authorities have opened an investigation. The attack marks the first strike on Egyptian soil since the US-Iran war began in February, and it carries profound strategic significance: Damietta is one of Egypt's two primary LNG export terminals, a facility that has served as a partial bypass route for European gas buyers locked out of Qatari LNG cargoes moving through the Hormuz corridor. If the attack signals the expansion of the energy-infrastructure campaign to Mediterranean port facilities, Europe's diversification strategy faces a serious new vulnerability — and the geopolitical risk premium, which today's pullback might suggest is deflating, could prove far more resilient than the tape implies.

On the Hormuz front, the first genuine glimmers of recovery emerged. Bloomberg, citing Kpler tanker-tracking data, reported on July 30 that 14 commodity vessel transits were counted on July 29 — up sharply from the near-zero readings of the preceding ten days and the first visible uptick since the July 7 Iranian vessel attacks reignited the crisis. Qatar dispatched its first LNG cargo through the strait since the conflict resumed, a symbolically important move even if physical volumes remain well below 20% of the pre-war baseline. Meanwhile, the US-Iran ceasefire pause — announced July 27 with Trump indicating talks could resume — has introduced the first diplomatic light since the June 17 ceasefire MOU collapsed. Analysts caution against over-reading either data point: 14 transits against a pre-war baseline of 88–100 per day represents only a modest improvement, and no formal negotiating framework is in place. August enters as a month of contested direction, with the Damietta attack and OPEC+ monitoring committee meeting (August 2) as the first tests.

Geopolitical Expansion

Drone Strikes Egypt's Damietta LNG Hub — US-Owned Vessels Hit in First Attack on Egyptian Soil Since War Began

A drone of unattributed origin struck two vessels at Egypt's Damietta Mediterranean port on July 29: the US-owned LNG storage vessel Energos Winter and the gas processing ship Gaslog Salem. Fires burned for hours before Egyptian emergency responders gained control. No group has claimed responsibility; both Iran and Houthi rebels denied involvement. Damietta has served as a partial bypass for European buyers unable to source Qatari LNG through the Hormuz corridor, making it a strategically critical facility. An attack that disrupts its operations would close a key European supply diversification route — adding a new layer of risk to an already severely stressed global LNG market and marking the first time the energy-infrastructure campaign has explicitly targeted a Mediterranean facility on Egyptian territory.

Hormuz Recovery

Hormuz Tanker Transits Tick Up to 14 on July 29 — Qatar Ships First LNG Cargo Through Strait Since Conflict Resumed

Bloomberg reported on July 30, citing Kpler tanker-tracking data, that 14 commodity vessels transited the Strait of Hormuz on July 29 — a meaningful improvement from the near-zero readings in the immediate aftermath of Iran's July 7 vessel attacks. Qatar, which had suspended LNG shipments through the strait following the escalation, dispatched its first LNG cargo since the resumption of hostilities. Analysts cautioned against premature optimism: 14 transits represent only about 14–16% of the pre-war 88–100 daily norm, and shipping insurers continue to charge war-risk premiums that make many voyages economically marginal. The recovery is real but extremely fragile — a single new incident in the strait could erase the week's progress within hours.

Corporate Earnings

Shell Posts Best Quarterly Profit in Four Years — $9.84 Billion Q2 Adjusted Earnings on War-Inflated Oil and LNG Prices

Shell reported Q2 2026 adjusted earnings of $9.84 billion on July 30, more than double year-ago levels and comfortably above analyst median forecasts of $8.79 billion — the company's best quarterly result in four years. The Hormuz crisis has proven a windfall for integrated energy majors: elevated crude benchmarks, record LNG spot prices, and near-capacity refinery utilization drove margins across all business segments simultaneously. Shell's refinery operations ran at close to maximum utilization as downstream crack spreads remain at historic highs. The result puts a sharp point on the distributional paradox of the crisis: while consumers face record pump prices, the upstream and integrated operators capturing crisis-level margins are posting profits not seen since the early post-COVID commodity surge.

Ceasefire / Diplomacy

US and Iran Pause Fighting — Trump Signals Talks Could Resume, But Tehran Says No Formal Negotiations Underway

On July 27, the United States halted new strikes on Iranian targets at Iran's request, and President Trump indicated he was open to resuming talks. Iran's foreign ministry, however, stated explicitly that no formal bilateral negotiations with the US were underway — a gap in framing that illustrates the fragility of the current pause. Oman-facilitated back-channel communications remain the only active diplomatic thread. The pause follows Trump's July 27 warning that the US would resume strikes without a new deal, and comes after the US revoked a 60-day Iranian oil sanctions waiver on July 8 following Iran's attack on three Hormuz vessels. The ceasefire pause is neither a ceasefire nor a deal — it is a cooling-off period whose durability depends on both sides finding sufficient incentive to avoid the next escalatory act.

05
OPEC+

OPEC+ Approved 188,000 bpd August Hike — Monitoring Committee Meets August 2 on September Targets as Hormuz Constrains Actual Flows

Seven core OPEC+ members — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — agreed on July 6 to raise collective output by 188,000 barrels per day effective August, the fifth consecutive monthly production increase and part of the gradual unwinding of the 2023 voluntary cut agreement. The group's monitoring committee meets August 2 to discuss September targets. In practice, the August hike is largely theoretical: with the Strait of Hormuz operating at well below 20% of its pre-war throughput of roughly 20 million barrels per day, Persian Gulf producers have no viable export route for incremental barrels regardless of paper quota levels. The August 2 meeting will be watched for any signal on whether the group pauses further increases in response to the sustained disruption — a pause would be modestly bullish by reducing one potential source of future supply.

EIA Inventories

EIA: 7.2-Million-Barrel Crude Draw for Week Ending July 24 — Stocks at 404.5 Million Barrels, 7% Below Five-Year Average

The EIA's weekly petroleum status report (week ending July 24, released July 29) confirmed a commercial crude draw of 7.2 million barrels — one of the largest draws of the year and nearly double analyst median expectations. Commercial crude stocks fell to 404.5 million barrels, 7% below the seasonal five-year average. Refineries operated at 97.2% capacity, one of the highest utilization rates on record, as processors raced to rebuild product inventories. Gasoline stocks added 0.9 million barrels but remain approximately 6% below seasonal averages, while distillate inventories rose 1.1 million barrels but stay about 9% below normal. The data confirms that US demand remained robust in the $85–$102 per barrel price band of the preceding two weeks — providing structural underpinning to any price pullback.

The Guru's Verdict — Friday, July 31, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

July closes with oil bulls bruised but unbroken. Brent's 2% retreat today to $85.10 is the kind of pullback that looks painful on the daily tape but registers as entirely healthy consolidation when you zoom out: the benchmark is still up roughly 19% for the month, still holding nearly all of Wednesday's extraordinary surge, and still supported by the same constellation of structural factors that have governed pricing for the past five weeks. Abqaiq is still offline. Hormuz is still operating at a fraction of normal capacity. US commercial crude stocks are 7% below the five-year seasonal average after a 7.2-million-barrel draw. None of those fundamentals changed today. What changed was the calendar — month-end position squaring and some profit-taking from traders who bought the mid-July lows under $80.

My read on today's Damietta development: The drone strike on Egypt's Damietta LNG hub is the most underappreciated risk event of the week. Markets are correctly focused on the Strait of Hormuz as the primary chokepoint, but Damietta represents the single most important bypass valve for European LNG buyers who have been routing demand away from Qatari cargoes since the Hormuz crisis began. If Damietta's export capacity is meaningfully curtailed — even temporarily — European gas markets face a secondary supply shock that the crude complex will eventually reprice upward. The market doesn't yet fully know what it's looking at with Damietta. That uncertainty is itself a reason to maintain a supply-risk bias rather than lean into today's pullback.

The bigger picture heading into August: We are entering a month of contested direction. The Hormuz recovery signal — 14 transits on July 29, Qatar's first LNG shipment through the strait — is real and, if it holds, represents the beginning of the supply normalization trade that the market has been anticipating since the June 15 peace deal announcement. But every previous attempt at normalization has been interrupted by escalation. The Damietta attack suggests that whatever actor is running the energy-infrastructure campaign is adapting its target set in response to Hormuz's partial recovery. The game is not over; it is expanding geographically. OPEC+ meets August 2 with the power to set the September quota tone. The Warsh FOMC decision on July 30 removed one key macro variable — the meeting is now behind us and the macro ceiling, whatever its precise height, is known. Supply uncertainty remains the dominant narrative.

My call: Brent holds $83–$91 next week, biased toward the lower half of that range in the very near term as month-end positioning unwinds, but with the structural bid intact and capable of reasserting sharply on any Damietta escalation, Abqaiq update, or ceasefire breakdown. Sell the spike, don't chase the dip — but recognize the floor is real and the tail risks are still skewed to the upside.
