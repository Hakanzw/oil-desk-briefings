⚡ Aftermath — Iran's SNSC Delivers Firm Rhetoric, No Military Retaliation; Brent Pulls Back from $90 Larak Peak

Tehran Condemns Larak Strike, Reaffirms Closed Hormuz Demands — Brent Retreats to $86.57 as Markets Price a Measured, Not Escalatory, Response

Iran's Supreme National Security Council, convened in emergency session after Sunday's US airstrike on Larak Island, delivered Tehran's formal response to Monday afternoon: a firm condemnation — loud enough to satisfy domestic audiences, restrained enough to leave the door to future diplomacy technically ajar. IRGC commanders called the strike an "illegal act of aggression" and pledged to "defend Iranian sovereignty by all necessary means," but stopped short of announcing specific retaliatory military action. Brent crude, which had surged to $90.25 at Monday's open on the immediate shock of the Larak raid — its highest in three weeks — retreated to $86.57/bbl on Tuesday morning, up just 0.94% on the day, as markets priced a posture that fell short of the active-escalation scenario traders had most feared.

Iran's measured tone does not change the fundamental arithmetic of the Hormuz crisis. The Strait — through which roughly one-fifth of global seaborne oil moved in normal times — remains effectively closed, with tanker transits holding at just 6–15 vessels per day against the 130+ daily baseline before hostilities erupted on February 28. Foreign Minister Araghchi reaffirmed the conditions for any reopening without revision: full US sanctions relief plus war reparations, demands the Trump administration has publicly rejected as non-starters. EIA data for the week ending August 21 confirms US distillate stocks remain 14% below the five-year seasonal average, and Asian importers — China, Japan, South Korea — have scrambled to reroute crude procurement toward the Atlantic Basin at significant logistical cost and freight-rate premium.

The Larak Island episode crystallizes the strategic paradox that has defined the market since February: the crisis is severe enough to prevent any orderly price decline, yet conditions fall short of the full-escalation scenario that would drive a sustained run toward $100. September begins with OPEC+ completing the final 188,000 bpd installment of its 2026 quota-restoration programme — a move that is largely symbolic while Hormuz-dependent barrels cannot physically reach market — and with Venezuela's potential OPEC exit adding a structural layer of organisational erosion to the supply picture. The EIA's August Short-Term Energy Outlook projects Brent averaging $85/bbl in Q3 2026, a forecast Tuesday's trading is currently tracking with precision. The risk premium is alive, recalibrated, and embedded in every barrel.

OPEC+ Supply

OPEC+ September Output Hike Takes Effect — Completing Full Rollback of All 2023 Voluntary Cuts

The final installment of OPEC+'s 2026 production-restoration schedule — 188,000 bpd for September — took effect on September 1, completing the phased reversal of all 1.65 million bpd of voluntary cuts the group agreed in 2023. Seven core members (Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman) ratified the increase at the August 2 ministerial meeting, with no further hikes planned for Q4 2026. As with every previous monthly increase this year, the paper quota substantially exceeds deliverable physical barrels: most Middle Eastern OPEC+ producers cannot route shipments through the near-closed Hormuz corridor, and several non-Gulf members face infrastructure constraints on expanding output to allotted levels. The group is expected to hold quotas steady through year-end, though that posture could change rapidly if diplomatic conditions shift.

Demand / Asia

Fuel Shock 2026: China, Japan, and South Korea Race to Secure Atlantic Basin Crude as Gulf Flows Collapse

China's seaborne crude imports fell 3.6 million b/d between February and April before partially recovering through Atlantic Basin suppliers — the United States, Brazil, Canada, and as far as Argentina. Japan and South Korea, which source between 75% and 90% of crude imports from the Middle East under normal conditions, have drawn down strategic petroleum reserves while securing emergency long-term contracts with non-Gulf producers. The supply reorientation has driven freight rates to multi-year highs and created logistical bottlenecks across Asian refining systems, contributing to global refined-product price volatility even as crude retreats from April's wartime peaks. Atlantic Basin crude exports to East-of-Suez destinations have surged by 3.5 million b/d since February — a structural shift in trade flows that will take years to unwind.

Inventories / EIA

EIA: Crude Stocks Edge Up 0.1 MB to 428.9 MB — Distillates 14% Below Seasonal Average; Refinery Runs Hit 97.4%

The EIA's Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week ending August 21 showed US commercial crude inventories rising a modest 0.1 million barrels to 428.9 million barrels — 1% above the five-year seasonal average. The slim crude build came as refineries operated at 97.4% of operable capacity, one of the highest utilization readings of the year. Despite robust processing runs, distillate fuel stocks fell 2.2 million barrels and remain 14% below the five-year seasonal average — a persistent structural concern as the autumn heating season approaches. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve stands at 289.7 million barrels (approximately 41% of capacity). The next inventory print, covering the week ending August 28, is due Wednesday, September 3.

OPEC / Geopolitics

Venezuela In Active OPEC Exit Deliberations — Trump Administration Eyeing US Stake in Its Oil Resources

Bloomberg reports Venezuela is in active internal consultations on leaving OPEC — the cartel it helped found in 1960 — as the Trump administration holds parallel talks about acquiring a US stake in the country's approximately 1.16 mb/d oil resource base. No final decision has been made, but a departure would follow the UAE's earlier 2026 withdrawal and would strip OPEC of roughly 5 million bpd of combined member production capacity — approximately 17% of the cartel's core member supply. The potential Venezuela exit compounds the structural credibility crisis facing OPEC at a moment when the Hormuz closure has already exposed the widening gap between paper quotas and physical deliverability. Markets are watching for a formal announcement that could arrive as early as September.

The Guru's Verdict — Tuesday, September 1, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

The market verdict on Tuesday is, in a phrase, relief without resolution. Iran's SNSC response to Larak was exactly what experienced Iran-watchers would have expected: harsh enough for domestic consumption, calibrated enough to avoid triggering a new round of US escalation before Tehran has fully consolidated its negotiating posture. The market correctly read the subtext. Brent retreated four dollars from Monday's spike — and then held. That hold is significant. The oil market is not treating $86 as a ceiling; it is treating it as a floor.

My read on today: The pullback from $90.25 to $86.57 is a healthy recalibration, not a trend reversal. The core equation has not shifted: Hormuz is effectively closed, distillate stocks remain 14% below seasonal norms, and OPEC+ is completing a quota-restoration cycle that cannot be physically delivered while the strait stays constrained. The EIA's August STEO projects Brent averaging $85/bbl in Q3 2026 — and Tuesday's price is almost precisely tracking that forecast. The risk premium built since February is not a spike; it is a structural feature of the market.

The bigger picture: September begins with three structural anchors that will define the next quarter: a Strait of Hormuz no closer to reopening under any foreseeable diplomatic scenario; an OPEC+ completing a quota-reversal cycle in an environment where those reversals are largely undeliverable to market; and a Venezuela exit story that, if confirmed, would mark the most consequential fracture in OPEC's organisational history since the 1970s oil shocks. All three are price-supportive. None are resolvable in the near term. The Oman diplomatic channel that briefly animated bullish sentiment has been reset to zero by Larak.

My call: Brent trades $83–$90 today, with the most likely range $85–$88 absent a new Iranian military action or a surprise diplomatic signal. The spike to $90 was real but fleeting; the floor at $83–$85 is structural, durable, and defended by every factor the market can currently see.
