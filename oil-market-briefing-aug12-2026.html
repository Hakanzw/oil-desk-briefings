Top Story

EIA Crude Draw Reverses Surprise Build — Brent Extends Six-Day Run Toward $90 as Hormuz Deadlock Deepens

Brent Pushes Toward $90 on EIA Draw Surprise and Sixth Day of Gains — Iran's Restrictive Navigation Draft Hardens the Standoff

Brent crude extended its winning streak to six consecutive sessions on Wednesday, climbing to ~$89.45/bbl — its highest level since before the June ceasefire repricing — after this morning's EIA weekly petroleum status report delivered a 1.7-million-barrel crude draw for the week ending August 7. The draw reversed the prior week's surprise 2.479-million-barrel build and came in squarely against consensus expectations of a small build of around 0.5 million barrels. Distillates — diesel and heating oil — remain 12% below the five-year seasonal average, a structural tightness that continues to underpin refinery margins even as upstream flows remain constrained by the Hormuz closure.

The diplomatic backdrop darkened further overnight. President Trump, in a Tuesday interview, added a new precondition to any deal: Iran must pay war reparations for Americans killed in attacks linked to the Islamic Republic. Tehran's position is the mirror image — Iran's Foreign Ministry insisted the U.S. must pay reparations as part of any framework. Meanwhile, Iran published a detailed navigation draft on Wednesday that would require all ships to request permission from Iranian authorities and sail close to the Iranian coastline while transiting the strait — conditions markets read as hardline and structurally difficult to impose on the broader shipping community. Analysts at CNBC warned that if the deadlock runs into next week without resolution, market moves will "not be so benign."

The cumulative effect of six days of gains — a +12% price run since the early-August low near $79/bbl — reflects accumulated risk premium rather than any fundamental supply recovery. Daily transits through the Strait of Hormuz remain at 8–15 vessels against a pre-war baseline of 130, and OPEC+ paper quota increases continue to be described by the Joint Ministerial Monitoring Committee itself as having a "negligible impact on the physical crude market" while the strait stays closed. Wednesday's EIA draw confirms the physical market is tight; the question for the desk is whether the $90 psychological resistance becomes a ceiling or a launching pad.

EIA Data

EIA: Crude Stocks Draw 1.7M Barrels — Reverses Last Week's Surprise Build; Distillates Remain 12% Below 5-Year Average

The U.S. Energy Information Administration's weekly petroleum status report, released Wednesday morning, showed a crude oil inventory draw of 1.7 million barrels for the week ending August 7 — against consensus expectations of a small build of roughly 0.5 million barrels and reversing the prior week's surprise 2.479-million-barrel build. Gasoline stocks also drew down, while distillate inventories remain approximately 12% below the five-year seasonal average at 107.2 million barrels — a structural deficit that keeps refinery margins elevated. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve has now fallen below 300 million barrels, its lowest level since 1983, as the Hormuz crisis continues to deplete emergency reserves.

Trump Adds War Reparations Demand — Iran Publishes Restrictive Hormuz Navigation Draft Requiring Ships to Seek Permission

The Hormuz negotiating gap widened Wednesday on two fronts. President Trump said Iran must pay war reparations for Americans killed in attacks linked to Tehran as a precondition for any deal — the mirror of Iran's own reparations demand on Washington. Separately, Iran published a draft Hormuz navigation framework that would require all ships to formally request permission from Iranian authorities and transit in a lane close to the Iranian coastline — conditions shipping analysts called "operationally unworkable" for the global tanker fleet. Markets had briefly rallied on Pakistan's defense minister saying the two sides were "close to some sort of arrangement"; the draft publication reversed that optimism. CNBC warned that a continued deadlock into next week "will not produce benign market moves."

OPEC+

OPEC+ Completes Voluntary Cut Rollback With September 188K bpd Hike — Group Signals Pause After Unwinding 1.65M bpd

Eight OPEC+ nations approved a 188,000 bpd production increase for September at their August 3 virtual meeting — the sixth consecutive monthly hike and the final tranche needed to unwind the 1.65 million bpd in additional voluntary cuts first introduced in April 2023. Saudi Arabia and Russia each absorb 62,000 bpd of the increase, with Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman sharing the remainder. Rystad Energy's Jorge Leon noted the group has "little incentive to rush further changes" and predicted a pause through Q4 2026 while members prepare 2027 quota negotiations. A separate, larger 2022 tranche of 2.0 million bpd in cuts remains in effect through end-2026. The OPEC+ JMMC acknowledged publicly that Hormuz constraints have made recent quota increases a "negligible" influence on physical supply.

Supply Risk

Houthis Strike Jizan Aramco Refinery Again — Fire Contained, No Casualties, But Infrastructure Complacency Flagged

Yemen's Houthi forces launched a drone attack on Saudi Aramco's Jizan refinery on Sunday, August 9 — the second major strike on the facility in weeks. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree confirmed the strike was "precise" and targeted the refinery complex directly. A fire broke out and was extinguished; Saudi Arabia reported no casualties from the refinery hit, though a concurrent Houthi attack on Yemen's al-Makha port killed seven people. Saudi Aramco CEO Amin Nasser acknowledged the attacks have caused "some production interruptions" but said operations remain recoverable with no material financial impact. Analysts warn the repeated "no operational impact" framing is creating complacency — a strike that knocks out actual production or damages a major export terminal would be an immediate, distinct shock on top of the Hormuz closure.

China Q2 Crude Imports Collapse 32% — Lowest Level Since 2016 as Hormuz Disruption Bites Beijing's Supply Chain

China's crude oil imports fell to 8.1 million barrels per day in Q2 2026 — a 32% drop quarter-over-quarter and the lowest import pace since 2016. The decline reflects the Hormuz crisis's direct impact on China's supply chain: waterborne arrivals from Iraq fell by 910,000 b/d, from Russia by 640,000 b/d, and from the UAE by 600,000 b/d — all major exporters whose cargoes typically transit the strait. Chinese refineries have been drawing down the enormous strategic reserves built in late 2025 (when Beijing aggressively stockpiled at $55–65/bbl) at a rate of 1.7 million b/d, contributing to global inventory draws of 5.1 million b/d in the quarter. The Chinese pullback has paradoxically moderated the price spike — reduced demand at high prices offsetting what could have been a far sharper supply squeeze.

The Guru's Verdict — Wednesday, August 12, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Two things happened today that pull in the same direction: a bullish EIA report and a tougher diplomatic picture. The 1.7-million-barrel crude draw reversed last week's jarring surprise build and took the "inventory demand destruction" narrative off the table for now. The distillate deficit — 12% below the five-year average — tells you the physical market is not finding equilibrium despite Chinese imports at decade lows. When that structural tightness combines with a Hormuz closure that is not visibly approaching resolution, you get what you have: a sixth consecutive day of gains and Brent knocking on $90.

My read on today: Iran's navigation draft is the most important new variable. Publishing a draft framework that requires ships to request permission and sail close to the Iranian coastline is not the behavior of a government about to reopen the strait. It is the behavior of a government institutionalizing its control of access — either as a bargaining position, or as the first draft of what a permanent Iranian toll regime might look like. The market is correctly treating it as a hardening signal. Trump's added reparations demand closes the public negotiating space further. Both sides are publicly demanding the other pay reparations — that is not a gap closable by the Oman channel alone.

The two-sided picture: The bearish scenario is a quiet back-channel concession that neither side announces publicly — perhaps a U.S. agreement to treat the navigation draft as a basis for negotiation, styled as a humanitarian gesture, while Iran quietly allows a few additional tankers through. That is not impossible; the June 15 deal surprised everyone. The bullish tail is the Iranian parliament scheduling the ship-ban vote, another Houthi strike that actually hits production, or Trump escalating the economic pressure with new sanctions. Any of those puts $93–$95 back on the board quickly. The EIA draw eliminates the one data-based reason to be bearish on physical tightness.

The call: Brent holds $88–$92 today, with $90 as the test. I am not chasing the sixth day of gains, but I am not fading the physical tightness either. The $90 level is psychological resistance, not fundamental resistance — the fundamentals, between a closed strait, a 12% distillate deficit, and a just-confirmed crude draw, argue for the upside of that range. Watch the Oman channel overnight and any Iranian parliamentary scheduling news. The next move is headline-driven from Tehran or Washington.
