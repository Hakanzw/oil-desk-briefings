⚡ UNGA Opens — Oil Slides for Fourth Straight Session as Iran Talks Loom

Trump Grants Tehran Visas, Threatens "Annihilation" in Same Breath — Crude Drops Below $102 as Traders Price UNGA Diplomacy Window

Crude oil fell for the fourth consecutive session on Monday, with Brent slipping to approximately $101.64 and WTI to $98.46, as markets assigned a growing probability to a diplomatic opening at this week's United Nations General Assembly in New York. The Trump administration approved travel visas for Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi to attend the UNGA high-level week — the first time senior Iranian officials have been permitted entry to the United States since the war erupted in February. The gesture, however modest in practical terms, has been interpreted by traders as the minimum necessary condition for a face-saving channel between the two combatants.

The diplomatic signal comes freighted with deliberate ambiguity. President Trump told Axios he faces a "big decision coming up" — framing his choices as either "going in and annihilating" Iran or pursuing a settlement — while senior U.S. officials briefed reporters that Iran "must never" obtain a nuclear weapon and warned Tehran of continued economic and military pressure. Trump is scheduled to deliver his UNGA address on Tuesday, September 22, with Iran as the centrepiece. Gulf Cooperation Council leaders are also gathering in New York for a summit with the president, amplifying the diplomatic intensity of the week. Whether this architecture produces even a preliminary framework, a ceasefire extension, or simply another escalatory press release remains entirely open.

Beneath the diplomatic noise, the physical supply picture has not materially improved. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline — which carries over 4 million barrels per day to the Red Sea at Yanbu — remains offline following drone-strike damage on September 11. Riyadh has redirected approximately 60 million barrels of crude marketed for September and October loadings through ship-to-ship transfer operations off Sohar, Oman, exploiting a narrow window outside Hormuz. But the logistical ceiling is real: total Middle East oil flows are running roughly 6.1 mb/d below 2025 averages at approximately 17.1 mb/d. Houthi forces, who have held Perim Island at the mouth of Bab el-Mandeb since September 12, issued a formal bar on Saudi-flagged vessels transiting the strait this past week — foreclosing Saudi Arabia's last conventional southern bypass. Any breakdown in this week's diplomacy would re-activate both chokepoints simultaneously.

Saudi Oil Exports Rebound Through Hormuz as East-West Pipeline Stays Offline

Saudi Aramco has redirected approximately 60 million barrels of crude for September and October loadings through ship-to-ship transfer operations at Sohar, Oman — just outside the Strait of Hormuz — following the September 11 drone strike on the East-West pipeline pumping station. Four supertankers were observed loading at Persian Gulf ports. The volumes are being absorbed primarily by refiners in China, South Korea, Japan and India. Experts expect the East-West pipeline to restart at 40–60% capacity within two to three weeks, restoring approximately 2.5–3 mb/d of Red Sea export flow.

Red Sea / Houthis

Houthis Strike Saudi Oil Facilities, Bar Saudi Vessels from Bab el-Mandeb

Houthi forces launched a major wave of ballistic missiles and drones at Saudi oil infrastructure in early September — striking Jazan's industrial zone, Aramco facilities in Najran and Abha, and King Khalid Air Base — wounding 73 people. The group has since formally barred Saudi-flagged vessels from transiting the Bab el-Mandeb strait and has held Perim Island at the strait's mouth since September 12. The closure eliminates Saudi Arabia's southern Red Sea export bypass and forces all Aramco volumes through the contested Hormuz corridor.

US Diesel Prices Hit Record $6.45/Gallon — Supply Chain Inflation Spreading

The average US retail diesel price climbed to a record $6.45 per gallon this week, a 74% surge over the year-ago level, according to AAA. Diesel — the fuel that moves goods, operates construction equipment, and underpins industrial logistics — has now set multiple all-time records since September: $5.85/gal on September 4, $6.05 on September 11, and $6.23 on September 14 before the latest peak. Economists warn the price spike travels rapidly from energy markets into freight costs, manufacturing inputs, and consumer inflation in ways that are harder to absorb than gasoline price shocks.

OPEC+

September 188k bpd Hike Completes OPEC+'s 2023 Voluntary Cut Rollback — Group Pauses Further Increases

OPEC+ implemented its final 188,000 bpd output increase in September, completing the phased rollback of the 1.65 million bpd voluntary cut package originally agreed in 2023. Core members — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Algeria, Kazakhstan and Oman — each absorbed a proportional share. The group will now pause further hikes and review the 2 million bpd in deeper cuts dating from 2022, which remain in place through year-end. A capacity review for 2027 quota baselines is ongoing. With Gulf output severely constrained by the war, the September increase is largely theoretical — actual production remains far below formal quotas.

The Guru's Verdict — Monday, September 21, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Four sessions of decline, two chokepoints, one summit. That is the arithmetic of Monday. Last week I wrote that the Brent retreat from $108.75 had been pricing diplomatic hope, not progress — and that remains the most honest description of where we are. Today's leg lower to approximately $101.64 is the market front-running the UNGA window: the visa grants to Pezeshkian and Araghchi, the scheduled Trump address on Tuesday, the GCC-Trump sideline meetings. None of that is a deal. All of it is a precondition for one. The gap between those two things is exactly where crude prices live right now.

My read today: The diplomatic compression is real but fragile. Trump's own framing — "annihilate them or reach a deal" — is not the language of a man who has decided. It is the language of a man who is still making the decision, and markets are right to price the optionality but wrong if they price resolution. The physical supply deficit has not improved by one barrel. Saudi Arabia is running 60 million barrels through a logistical workaround that depends on clear passage through a strait that Iran effectively commands. The East-West pipeline is offline for at least two more weeks. Houthis have shut the southern bypass. The structural supply case for triple-digit crude has not changed — only the diplomatic mood.

The bigger picture: The distillate story is becoming its own crisis. Diesel at $6.45 per gallon — an all-time record — is not simply an energy price number; it is the inflation transmission mechanism for the entire goods economy. Freight costs are rising, manufacturing inputs are rising, and the political pressure on the administration to end the war is building from constituencies beyond the foreign policy establishment. That political pressure is both the bullish and bearish case: it could accelerate a deal, or it could push Trump toward an escalatory military option that forecloses diplomacy for months. Both tails are fatter than the mid-point suggests.

My call: Brent $99–$108 for the week, with resolution likely in Tuesday's tape after Trump's UNGA address. Bias today is cautiously lower into the diplomatic window — a constructive UNGA tone could test $99–$100. But I hold both tails wide open: a hostile Trump address or a Houthi interdiction of a Saudi cargo in the Bab el-Mandeb approaches would re-light the upside toward $110+ in a single session. This market is not trending — it is waiting.
