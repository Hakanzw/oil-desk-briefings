Weekly Wrap — Supply Return Dominates: Ras Tanura Restarts, Hormuz Traffic Surges, Oman Incident Tests the Floor

Oil Posts a Fourth Consecutive Weekly Loss as Saudi Ras Tanura Resumes Exports and Hormuz Traffic Surges — Cargo Ship Strike Near Oman Provides a Fleeting Floor Before Markets Refocus on Supply Recovery

The week that began with WTI below $70 and Brent at a two-month low ended with the most concrete evidence yet that the Gulf's post-ceasefire supply recovery is not merely a headline — it is a fact on the water. Saudi Aramco's Ras Tanura export terminal, idle since early March, resumed operations this week, with multiple VLCCs spotted loading crude at the Ju'aymah offshore buoys — the first tankers to take on Saudi crude at the Kingdom's primary Gulf terminal in nearly four months. Brent settled at $74.43 (−1.11%) on Friday, capping a week in which crude shed roughly 4% from Monday's open as markets priced the systematic unwinding of the war premium. WTI settled around $71.26 (−1.10%), hovering above the $70 threshold that Thursday's session briefly pierced. Against this backdrop, Friday produced a sharp intraday footnote: a cargo ship was struck by a projectile near Oman, triggering a brief ~2% spike in Brent before traders read the context — the ceasefire held, Iran denied involvement, and the vessel was not carrying crude — and reversed the move entirely. The incident was a vivid illustration of the new market psychology: geopolitical noise without a direct supply implication is now being sold.

The Ras Tanura resumption is the supply milestone the market has been waiting for. Saudi Aramco's actual production during the Hormuz closure ran roughly 7.7 mb/d below its OPEC+ quota of 10.29 mb/d as loading and export operations were suspended. The terminal's return changes the physical equation: Gulf producers can now actually move crude, not merely lift OPEC+ quotas on paper. Iraq, Kuwait, and Iran are simultaneously ramping export operations as stranded tankers clear Hormuz. An ING analyst note captured the transition plainly: "Once the vessels now moving through the Strait have cleared the backlog queue, the structural flow rate — not the surge — will tell us what this market is actually worth." That reckoning arrives with the July 2 EIA weekly report, the first to capture post-reopening US crude import data for the week ending June 26. Three consecutive draws totaling over 10 million barrels kept the bearish narrative partly restrained; if July 2 shows the first material build, the downside case for Brent toward $70 accelerates significantly.

Hormuz transit data for the June 19–21 weekend confirmed 93 vessel transits — nearly three times the 32 recorded the prior weekend — with estimated flows reaching 12 million barrels per day. The recovery is real but incomplete: pre-war Hormuz flows ran at approximately 20 mb/d, covering roughly one-fifth of global seaborne oil. Iranian mines remain in the Strait; the US mine-clearance operation begun April 11 carries an estimated 3–6 month full clearance timeline, meaning physical recovery will be staged across the remainder of 2026. This combination — surging near-term transit, persistent mine risk, unresolved IAEA nuclear inspection dispute, and the Treasury general license expiring August 21 — constitutes the market's live risk register. Natural gas tells a separate story: Henry Hub eased 0.92% to $3.22/MMBtu on Friday, but Qatar's Ras Laffan damage assessment is still ongoing, and any structural damage findings at the world's largest LNG complex would rapidly reverse the risk bid that has supported the complex since the June 21–22 explosion.

Saudi Ras Tanura Roars Back: First VLCCs Load at Ju'aymah Since March — Gulf Export Infrastructure Returns

In the most concrete supply milestone of the post-ceasefire period, Saudi Aramco's Ras Tanura export terminal resumed operations this week, with multiple Very Large Crude Carriers loading at the Ju'aymah offshore buoys for the first time since early March. Iraq, Kuwait, and Iran have simultaneously resumed or accelerated export operations as the Strait of Hormuz clears. Middle East fuel oil exports are separately forecast to hit a four-month high in coming weeks, providing a leading indicator for crude recovery: fuel oil clears shorter transits first, crude throughput follows within weeks.

The market is treating the ceasefire as the dominant narrative and near-term incidents as noise. The incident nonetheless underscores that mine risk and non-state actor activity in the Arabian Sea remain live dimensions of the region's security profile, and that any future event directly implicating Iran or crude flows would behave very differently.

US Gas Prices Fall for Sixth Straight Week — But July 4 National Average Still Forecast at $3.75/Gallon, 65¢ Above Last Year

US retail gasoline prices have declined for six consecutive weeks as crude's post-ceasefire retracement filters through to the pump — the longest sustained decline since 2022. But the relief is incomplete: GasBuddy forecasts a national average of $3.75 per gallon on July 4, which would be the second-highest Independence Day pump price on record and 65 cents above last year. The gap between crude's dramatic retracement and retail fuel prices reflects the structural lag in downstream markets and the ongoing constraint from refinery throughput — the IEA projects global refinery throughput at 82 mb/d in 2026, down 2 mb/d from pre-conflict levels, including the lasting impact of destroyed Russian refining capacity. Consumers will feel the war's energy cost well into the summer driving season even as crude futures approach pre-conflict levels.

UK Energy Secretary Vetoes Plan to Increase North Sea Oil Output for Defense Spending — Energy vs. Security Battle Intensifies

UK Energy Secretary Ed Miliband rejected a Treasury proposal to expand North Sea drilling to generate revenue earmarked for defense spending — a decision that has reignited political tensions between the UK's net-zero commitments and its defense funding demands. The Treasury argument: additional hydrocarbon extraction could generate billions of pounds in licensing fees and tax receipts at a time when NATO commitments strain government finances. Miliband's counter: domestic oil output has a multi-year lead time, cannot close a near-term defense funding gap, and would compromise the UK's energy transition credibility. The veto marks a significant political flashpoint at the intersection of two of Europe's most pressing policy debates — energy security and defense spending — and is being watched closely by other European governments navigating the same tradeoffs in a post-conflict energy landscape.

Regional fuel oil exports from the Middle East are forecast to reach their highest levels in four months in coming weeks as Hormuz shipping conditions improve and war-risk insurance premiums for tanker operators begin to decline meaningfully. The export surge reflects two simultaneous forces: producers physically able to move product again through a reopening Strait, and a risk-premium reduction that makes Gulf shipments economically viable for charterers who had been routing cargoes around the Arabian Sea or paying premium rates. VLCC daily earnings remain elevated at approximately $470,000 — well above pre-conflict norms — but the trajectory is unambiguously lower. Fuel oil exports are a leading indicator for crude: fuel oil moves before crude in recovery dynamics because it clears on shorter transits; the crude volume follow-through is measured in weeks, not days, and the July–August loading programs now in the market will tell the full story.

The Guru's Verdict — Friday, June 26, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Friday's close draws a line under the most dramatic week in post-war crude pricing: Brent at $74.43, WTI at $71.26, and the war premium that ruled this market for four months now approximately 48% erased. The Saudi Ras Tanura resumption is the data point of the week — not a press release or a diplomatic statement, but a VLCC at a loading buoy taking on crude for the first time since March. When the world's largest oil exporter puts physical barrels on tankers again after a three-month halt, the market has no choice but to price it. And it has. What's equally notable is what Friday's Oman vessel incident tells us: in any week from March to May, a ship strike in the Arabian Sea would have sent crude sharply higher and held it there. Today, the move was fully reversed within hours. The geopolitical risk premium is gone. The market is in supply-return mode.

My read on Friday: The immediate question is what the July 2 EIA report says. Three consecutive crude draws kept the bearish narrative partly in check; a first material build confirms that Gulf barrels are arriving at US shores. If the week ending June 26 shows a build of 4+ million barrels, Brent's $70 support becomes the next live test. If it shows a fourth draw — still possible, given that Ras Tanura only just restarted and VLCC transit times to the US are 20–35 days — the downside is more gradual but remains the directional bias. The market is not trying to find the bottom. It is pricing a structural return of supply with rational patience. The Oman incident today was the market's chance to flinch — and it didn't. That tells you everything about where sentiment sits.

The bigger picture: Two structural forces now compete for the post-war crude narrative. The bearish case — Ras Tanura back, Hormuz flowing, Iranian crude on the market, OPEC+ quotas being restored — is winning decisively on near-term price action. The bullish tail — unresolved IAEA dispute, mine risk in Hormuz, Qatar LNG uncertainty, Russia diesel ban risk, and the ever-present possibility of a ceasefire wobble before August 21 — is not being priced. That asymmetry matters. At $74 Brent, the market is offering almost no insurance against scenarios where the supply return stalls or reverses. Whether that is rational pricing of a durable diplomatic architecture or collective complacency is the judgment call every crude trader has to make heading into the weekend.

My call: Brent tests the $70–$72 range over the next 1–2 weeks as the July 2 EIA report is likely to show the first meaningful crude builds as Gulf imports begin to arrive. The floor — set by OPEC+ fiscal breakeven logic and Saudi Arabia's demonstrated capacity to signal — sits near $70 WTI / $73 Brent. A break below that floor requires either an OPEC+ surprise or a genuinely large EIA build. Above the current range, any escalation in the IAEA dispute, a mine incident in Hormuz, or a Qatar damage-assessment disappointment could briefly reverse 2–3 weeks of decline in a single session. Sell the geopolitical spikes; respect the OPEC+ floor — and watch the July 2 EIA report above all else this week.
