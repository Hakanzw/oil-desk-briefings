⚡ Wednesday Market — The Clock Runs Out: Ceasefire Expires, Trump Confirms No Iran Talks, Hormuz Preconditions Unchanged

60-Day US-Iran Ceasefire Lapses Without Extension; Trump Says No Talks Are Ongoing — Brent Rises for a Fourth Straight Session as the Last Formal Diplomatic Framework Dissolves

The last formal structure holding open a diplomatic channel between Washington and Tehran expired Tuesday night — and nobody blinked. The 60-day US-Iran ceasefire, negotiated in June and already described by this desk as increasingly nominal, lapsed without an extension agreement, and President Trump made the market's worst-case scenario explicit on Tuesday: "There are no talks ongoing with Tehran." Brent crude rose for a fourth consecutive session, opening Wednesday near $91.32; WTI held above $85. The four-session winning streak has added roughly 4.5% to crude prices since last Thursday, all of it driven by the progressive collapse of any diplomatic pathway back to normalized Hormuz traffic.

Iran's response arrived simultaneously in two registers. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi maintained the preconditions he set last week — comprehensive sanctions relief and war reparations before any Hormuz reopening — while unnamed Iranian officials signaled Tehran may shift to an "offensive policy" if diplomacy fails entirely. That pairing — unchanged preconditions plus an escalation signal — describes a government that sees no incentive to moderate its position. Against a backdrop where Hormuz remains at 8–15 vessel transits per day versus roughly 130 before the war, and where 410 million barrels of global inventory have been drawn down since the conflict began in February, the structural supply picture grows worse each week the diplomatic stalemate persists.

Today's EIA weekly petroleum status report — covering the week ending August 15 — is the first inventory print fully inside the period Defense Secretary Hegseth declared the blockade "indefinite." The critical watch is the export line: the prior report (week ending August 7) showed a record 17.4 million barrel crude build driven by a surge in imports and a collapse in exports, a signature of the Hormuz Paradox where domestic crude accumulates because Gulf-bound tankers cannot move. If today's report shows the paradox reversing — a crude draw alongside rising exports — it signals non-Gulf barrels are beginning to displace Gulf supply in the Atlantic Basin. If the paradox continues, the structural tightness in refined products — gasoline 6% below the five-year average, distillates 12% below — will deepen further.

IEA August OMR

IEA: Global Inventories Down 410 Million Barrels From Pre-War Levels — Lowest Since April 2025; World 'Burning Through Stockpiles'

The IEA's August Oil Market Report confirmed that global observed inventories now sit 410 million barrels below pre-war levels — their lowest since April 2025 — as the Hormuz disruption prevents supply from reaching markets. Global oil supply is projected to decline 4.3 mb/d in 2026, with Gulf shut-ins averaging 8.3 mb/d at peak in July. Global inventories fell 69 million barrels in July alone. Demand is simultaneously projected to fall 1.6 mb/d from price-induced destruction. Atlantic Basin refining margins hit all-time highs in July as diesel, jet fuel, and gasoline shortfalls collided with refineries already running at near-ceiling utilization — the physical tightness that the ceasefire expiration has just made structurally worse.

Supply Outlook

US Government Estimates Hormuz Disruptions Will Persist Through 2027 — Averaging ~600,000 bpd of Effective Shut-In Supply

US government analysts now estimate Hormuz-related supply disruptions will average approximately 600,000 bpd through the end of 2027 — effectively ruling out any near-term normalization of Gulf oil flows. The estimate accounts not just for the physical blockade but for the mine-clearing timeline, the time required to restart idled Gulf production fields, and the assumed duration of a negotiated settlement process. That settlement process, following the ceasefire expiration and Trump's Tuesday statement, now has no visible starting point. The US government's own estimate has become the market's base case: the disruption is structural, not episodic.

OPEC+ / Supply

OPEC+ Confirms Q4 Production Pause After Completing Full Voluntary Cut Rollback — September Hike Was the Last

OPEC+ confirmed on August 2 that its 188,000 bpd September production increase completes the full rollback of the 1.65 mb/d in voluntary cuts agreed in 2023. The group has confirmed a Q4 pause on further output increases while conducting a 2027 capacity review. A separate layer of approximately 2 million bpd in 2022-vintage cuts remains in place through end-2026. Critically, analysts note that the successive monthly output hikes throughout 2026 have been largely nominal: Gulf and Caspian disruptions mean those incremental barrels have never physically reached the market. The group has now exhausted its most flexible pricing instrument, leaving the September 6 JMMC as the next formal policy signal.

Refining / Products

Atlantic Basin Refining Margins Hit All-Time Highs — NYMEX 3-2-1 Crack Spread Reached $64.58/bbl in July as Product Shortfalls Deepen

Atlantic Basin refining margins reached all-time highs in July and remain historically elevated in August as diesel, jet fuel, and gasoline shortfalls collide with refineries already running at near-ceiling utilization. The NYMEX 3-2-1 crack spread — the standard US refinery profitability benchmark — reached $64.58 per barrel in early July, a level without historical precedent. US refinery utilization is currently at 96.5%, near the operational ceiling. Product shortfalls are now the downstream expression of the Hormuz supply shock: gasoline is 6% below the five-year seasonal average; distillates are 12% below, the widest deficit in the current data series. The ceasefire expiration removes any near-term catalyst for relief.

The tape is now trading without any diplomatic ceiling. The four-session winning streak has added 4.5% to crude. The question today is not whether the structure has changed — it has — but whether the market has finished repricing it.

My read on today: The ceasefire expiration was visible on the calendar for weeks. What the market is repricing is the confirmation of worst-case: no extension, no talks, Iran layering in escalation language. Every prior diplomatic near-miss — the Qatar back-channel signals, the 'advanced stage' talk reports that proved empty five times since June — has taught the tape to wait for confirmation before moving. Today's session is that confirmation. $91 is not a ceiling. It is where the market is standing while it figures out what comes next.

The structural picture is the most unambiguous it has been. Global inventories are 410 million barrels below pre-war levels and at their lowest since April 2025. US refinery utilization is at 96.5% with distillates 12% below the five-year average. The only live question is whether today's EIA report shows the Hormuz Paradox reversing — and even if it does, the ceasefire expiration means the underlying supply shortfall will not resolve on its own.

My call: Brent anchors $91–$95 today, with the bias tilted firmly higher now that the last diplomatic framework has dissolved and Iran is signaling escalation. The watch level above is $95–$97 — the zone that opens on a formal Iranian military escalation statement or early Hormuz bill passage. The watch level below is $89–$91, accessible only on a credible surprise diplomatic signal that neither side is currently positioned to give. The ceasefire is over. The exit is closed. Watch the EIA export line and Iran's next formal statement — those are today's two pivots.
