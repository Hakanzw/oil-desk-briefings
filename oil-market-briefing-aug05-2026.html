Top Story

⚡ Deal Window Opens — Bessent Names 24 Hours; Qatar Drafts Proposal; Iran Leadership Approves

'We May Have a Deal Today or Tomorrow': Oil Plunges Below $80 as Iran Approves Hormuz Corridor Plan, Qatar Confirms Draft Proposal, Bessent Names a 24-Hour Window

The Hormuz peace trade entered its most concrete phase yet on Wednesday, as three independent confirmation signals converged to price in an imminent deal. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC Tuesday that "there is a chance we may have a deal today or tomorrow to open the strait and move towards a more normalized position," adding that "hundreds of ships are waiting to go out." Secretary of State Marco Rubio echoed the language. Qatar, serving as a key intermediary, confirmed that an interim proposal had been drafted and that both Washington and Tehran were signaling progress. Most decisively, a US official and a regional source told Reuters that Iranian leadership had completed its internal approval process on Tuesday. Oil responded without hesitation: Brent plunged more than 5% to approximately $79.50 per barrel — its first breach of the $80 threshold since July 13 — while WTI fell 5.5% to $75.77, both benchmarks now down more than 10% over two sessions.

The structure taking shape is a 60-day temporary arrangement between Oman and Iran to establish a combined safe-shipping corridor through the Strait of Hormuz — merging Oman's southern passage with Iran's northern transit lane into a single round-trip corridor. This is not a full Hormuz reopening, which would require a US-Iran ceasefire agreement and mine-clearing operations that US commanders have told Congress could take up to six months. Instead, it is an operational workaround: a managed partial solution that could restore incremental commercial navigation without dissolving the broader conflict. The diplomatic consensus supporting it is unusually broad — Qatar drafted the proposal, Saudi Arabia and the UAE reportedly urged Trump last weekend to delay strikes to allow the corridor talks to proceed, and Oman has been directly mediating the technical parameters throughout. Trump extended his Iran deal deadline rather than authorising new strikes, preserving military optionality while the corridor agreement matures.

Two maritime incidents on Tuesday-Wednesday complicated the picture without fully derailing it. The Indian-flagged cargo vessel MSV Faize Noore Oliya, carrying oil from Saudi Arabia, sank in the Red Sea off Yemen after a projectile strike approximately 13 nautical miles off Hodeidah — all 14 crew members were rescued. Separately, an unknown projectile struck a cargo ship inside the Strait of Hormuz itself on Tuesday, with no party claiming responsibility. The EIA's weekly petroleum status report, released Wednesday, added a further unexpected variable: US commercial crude oil inventories posted a surprise build — the first in six weeks — as Cushing, Oklahoma rose and diplomatic optimism tempered the pace of spot crude purchasing, even while gasoline and distillate stocks remained approximately 6–9% below their five-year seasonal averages. The convergence of a deal-pricing market, active maritime risk, and a cracking tightness thesis defines Wednesday's session.

Surprise EIA Crude Build — First in Six Weeks; Cushing Rises as Diplomatic Optimism Tempers Spot Buying

The EIA's weekly petroleum status report, released Wednesday August 5 for the week ending July 31, showed a surprise build in US commercial crude oil inventories — the first weekly increase in six weeks — as Cushing, Oklahoma hub storage rose approximately 2.4 million barrels and diplomatic optimism reduced the urgency of spot crude purchasing. Gasoline inventories remained approximately 6% below the five-year seasonal average, while distillate stocks were roughly 9% below. The build represents the first credible challenge to the supply-tightness thesis that has underpinned the price floor throughout the Hormuz disruption: if stocks are growing before the corridor is even operational, the physical-market floor may be softer than six weeks of consecutive draws implied. OPEC+

OPEC+ September 188,000 bpd Hike Confirmed — Year-End Production Pause Provides Structural Floor for Gradual Hormuz Recovery

OPEC+'s voluntary-cut bloc confirmed the 188,000 barrel-per-day September output increase, completing the phased rollback of the April 2023 voluntary reductions. Sources previously told Reuters and Bloomberg the group has agreed in principle to pause all further output increases through year-end after September. The significance of the pause has grown sharper as Brent approaches $79: if a Hormuz corridor materialises and barrels begin returning gradually, they will arrive into a market where OPEC+ is holding production flat — limiting the pace of any supply-surplus build and establishing a medium-term floor the market can trade against. The pause does not prevent emergency action if Brent falls below a tolerated floor, which analysts estimate in the mid-$70s for Saudi Arabia's fiscal breakeven.

Brent Below $80 Activates Asian Demand Floor — India and China Lift Spot Purchasing as Sub-$80 Trigger Kicks In

The slide below $80 per barrel activated a well-telegraphed demand-side response from Asian buyers. India and China — which together account for the majority of incremental global oil demand growth in 2026 — have standing guidance from their state refiners to increase spot crude purchases when Brent trades below $80. India has recently overtaken China as the top demand-growth driver, adding approximately 130,000 bpd year-on-year; Indian state refiners including IOC and BPCL had signalled readiness to increase volumes at sub-$80 prices. Global petroleum consumption tracks toward 104.2 million barrels per day for 2026, meaning recovered Hormuz barrels arrive into a market with limited structural slack — the demand floor below $80 historically limits downside to the mid-$70s without a dramatic and sustained throughput normalization.

The Guru's Verdict — Wednesday, August 5, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Wednesday's oil market is trading the deal before the deal exists. Brent at $79.50 and WTI at $75.77 represent a complete erasure of the past two weeks' war-premium reconstruction — the exact trade the June 15 'deal complete' declaration produced in a single session, and which was fully reversed within six weeks. The difference today is the specificity of the signals: it is not a single presidential announcement from the Oval Office. It is Bessent naming a 24-hour window on CNBC, Qatar confirming a drafted proposal, and a regional source telling Reuters that Iran's leadership has completed its approval process — three independent confirmation points from distinct channels, each corroborating the others. The market is treating them as qualitatively superior to anything it has seen since February. The question is no longer whether the press conference happens. It is whether the corridor moves a tanker.

My read on today: The Oman-Iran corridor structure is actually more implementable than a full ceasefire — and that is why it is moving faster. It does not require mine-clearing. It does not require a formal US-Iran diplomatic agreement. It requires only Iran's operational consent and Oman's logistical cooperation to designate a combined southern-northern transit lane. If it materialises and tanker transits move from 14 per day to even 30, the supply-tightness thesis takes a serious hit — not a rout, but a structural downward reprice that the EIA build today amplifies: if stocks are building before the corridor is even operational, the physical floor is softer than six weeks of draws implied. I am revising my near-term supply-tightness read from 'persisting' to 'cracking.' The mid-$70s, which I treated as a tail scenario for a clean signing last week, is now the central scenario for a working corridor.

The bigger picture: The two maritime strikes — an Indian tanker in the Red Sea and a projectile in the strait itself — are the market's insurance policy against over-confidence. Neither has been claimed. Neither has escalated. But they demonstrate that the physical operating environment remains adversarial even as diplomatic calendars fill with deal language. The Oman corridor makes maritime insurance premiums affordable again; it does not guarantee safe passage without active security arrangements on the water. I am watching for two things Thursday: (1) the first transit-count data point under any new corridor arrangement, and (2) whether either incident is attributed to the IRGC — because that attribution changes the entire diplomatic calculus before ink dries on any agreement. The deal trades like a deal. The strait trades like a conflict. Both are true simultaneously.

My call: Brent $74–$82 today, bias lower toward a deal announcement, with violent upside tail if either maritime incident escalates or the Oman corridor talks stall. The market has moved aggressively to price an imminent deal — a clean corridor announcement pushes Brent toward $74–76; failure to deliver by Thursday's Asia open triggers a sharp reversal toward $83–85. This is the highest-conviction directional session of the past six weeks. Respect the signals. But remember that the last time this many credible sources confirmed a 'complete' deal, oil was back at $88 within a month. The corridor must move tankers, not press releases.
