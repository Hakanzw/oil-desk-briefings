Top Story

Hormuz Stalemate — Iranian FM Sets Hard Conditions for Strait Reopening

Oil Climbs as Iran Demands End to U.S. Naval Blockade and War Reparations Before Hormuz Can Reopen

Brent crude rose to ~$84.43/bbl in early Monday trade — up more than 1% at 4:30 GMT — after Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi delivered conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz that Washington is unlikely to meet quickly. Speaking over the weekend, Araghchi said Tehran would not restore normal transit until the United States ends its naval blockade, eases sanctions, and provides war reparations for damage inflicted by the February 28 Operation Epic Fury strikes. The demands widen what markets had hoped was a narrowing negotiating gap, injecting fresh premium into crude just as analysts had begun pricing a gradual resolution.

The Hormuz crisis remains the defining force in global oil markets. Before the conflict, the strait carried roughly 17 million barrels per day — about 20% of seaborne supply. Today only 8–15 vessels transit daily versus the pre-war average of 130, a choke so severe that the disruption has permanently warped global shipping routes. Iran's parliament is additionally reviewing a bill that would formally ban ships linked to the U.S. and Israel from using the strait entirely and impose tolls on other vessels — legislation that, if passed, would codify the blockade as Iranian law and complicate any near-term diplomatic resolution. Analysts at Rystad and JPMorgan noted that "lingering questions about practical details of any agreement" are the main mechanism keeping Brent elevated well above its pre-conflict mid-$70s baseline.

Compounding the geopolitical noise, Houthi forces in Yemen launched a drone strike on the Saudi Aramco refinery in Jizan on Saturday, lighting a fire that Saudi authorities said was swiftly extinguished with no casualties or material operational impact. The strike — alongside a separate Houthi attack on al-Makha port that killed seven Saudi soldiers — underscores that the region's proxy conflict is still very much active even as U.S.–Iran diplomatic channels remain open. For the oil desk, the Jizan hit was a reminder that the risk premium is not purely a Hormuz story: Saudi export infrastructure is in the crosshairs too.

Houthis Strike Aramco Refinery in Jizan — Saudi Arabia Says Fire Out, No Operational Impact

Iran / Policy

Iran Parliament Reviews Bill to Formally Ban U.S. and Israeli Ships and Levy Tolls on Others

Iran's parliament is considering legislation that would permanently ban ships linked to the United States and Israel from transiting the Strait of Hormuz and require all other vessels to pay a toll. The Trump administration rejected the proposal. If passed, the bill would codify the effective blockade into Iranian law, making any diplomatic reopening legally and politically far harder. The measure reflects hardliner pressure in Tehran to extract maximum leverage from Iran's control of the world's most critical oil chokepoint.

OPEC+

OPEC+ Completes Full Rollback of Voluntary Cuts — 188k bpd Added for September

The seven-nation OPEC+ core group on August 3 approved a final 188,000 bpd production increase for September, completing the full restoration of the 1.65 million bpd in voluntary cuts introduced in April 2023. Rystad Energy analyst Jorge Leon noted "the next challenge is managing the surplus that could emerge as export flows normalise." A separate 2022 tranche of ~2 million bpd in cuts remains in place through end-2026. The next ministerial monitoring committee meets September 6.

Demand

China's Crude Imports Plunge to Lowest Since 2016 — Down 41% Year-on-Year in Q2

China imported just 8.1 million barrels per day of crude in Q2 2026, down 41% year-on-year and 32% quarter-on-quarter — the lowest level since 2016 — as elevated Hormuz-driven prices throttled purchases. The largest supply drops came from Iraq (−910,000 b/d), Russia (−640,000 b/d), and the UAE (−600,000 b/d). China's demand destruction is simultaneously moderating the price spike that would otherwise accompany such a severe supply disruption, keeping Brent from revisiting the March–April war-premium peaks.

EIA: Surprise 2.5M-Barrel Crude Build Last Week — Gasoline and Distillates Both Drew Down

The EIA's weekly report for the period ending July 31 showed a surprise crude build of 2.479 million barrels against a consensus forecast of a 1.5 million barrel draw — a sharp reversal from the prior week's 7.167 million barrel draw. Refineries ran at 96.5% utilization. Despite the crude build, gasoline stocks fell 1.6 million barrels and distillates dropped 3.5 million barrels — the latter now sitting roughly 12% below the five-year average, a level that points to persistent tightness in diesel and heating-oil markets. The next EIA report is due August 12.

The Guru's Verdict — Monday, August 10, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Iran's Foreign Minister entered the weekend with a list of demands — end the blockade, lift sanctions, pay reparations — and left with those demands intact. That is the oil market's single most important data point today. For three weeks the desk has been watching the Oman-brokered negotiation for any sign that the gap between Washington and Tehran was narrowing enough to loosen the Strait of Hormuz. What we got instead was a codification of Iran's opening position, delivered in public, with a parliamentary bill waiting in the wings that would convert the blockade into law.

My read on today: Brent's +0.67% gain to ~$84 is orderly and entirely coherent with the news. The market is not pricing a new escalation — it is repricing a delayed resolution. The gap between the EIA's $74/bbl Q3 forecast (published before Iran's weekend statement) and the current $84 spot reflects exactly the risk premium the market is rationally maintaining: Hormuz is still shut, Araghchi just said it stays shut until conditions that Washington cannot quickly meet are satisfied, and Iran's parliament may enshrine that position in law.

The two-sided picture: The bearish case requires believing that the U.S. quietly concedes on some subset of Iran's demands — most plausibly a symbolic softening on the naval blockade — within the next few sessions. That is not impossible; back-channel deals have surprised markets before. The bullish tail is the Iran parliament passing the ship-ban bill, a Houthi strike that actually knocks out Saudi production, or a breakdown in Oman talks that sends Araghchi home. Any of those puts $90+ squarely back on the board. China's demand destruction — imports at a 10-year low — is the one structural buffer preventing a return to the March shock levels.

The call: Brent holds $82–$87 today, with the midpoint around $84–$85 as the base case if no headline breaks. I am not chasing the rally but I am not fading it either — Iranian conditions must credibly narrow before the risk premium justifies unwinding. Watch the Oman channel, the parliamentary vote calendar, and Wednesday's EIA print. The next meaningful move is headline-driven, not data-driven.
