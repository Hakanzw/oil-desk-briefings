⚡ Breaking — Ceasefire Pause: Oil Retreats From $102 High, But Houthi Strikes on Aramco Keep a Floor

US and Iran Halt Military Strikes — Brent Falls Below $92 as Weekend Houthi Attacks on Saudi Aramco Complicate the Selloff

The trade that drove Brent above $100 for the first time since May snapped violently over the weekend. The United States and Iran announced a mutual pause in military strikes on Sunday, July 26 — the first suspension since hostilities resumed in early July after the June ceasefire collapsed. The announcement triggered an immediate selloff: Brent crude fell 7.56% to $90.95 on Sunday and extended declines at Monday's open before partially recovering to around $92 — approximately 10% below the $102 peak reached just four days earlier on July 23. WTI fell to as low as $83.80 before steadying in the mid-$80s.

The picture is sharply complicated by simultaneous Houthi escalation in a new theater. On Friday July 25, Houthi forces struck Saudi Aramco's Jizan refinery complex, triggering a large fire visible from offshore. A follow-on ballistic missile salvo targeting Aramco's Yanbu facility was intercepted Sunday by a Patriot battery before causing damage. The dual-strike campaign represents the most serious sustained threat to Saudi oil export infrastructure since the 2019 Abqaiq attack and adds a second chokepoint risk — the Bab al-Mandeb — alongside the already-constrained Strait of Hormuz, which is running at only ~25 ships per day versus 130 pre-war.

Traders are navigating a genuinely two-sided tape. The US-Iran pause removes the most acute escalation risk and theoretically opens a path toward Hormuz re-normalization. But the pause carries no formal terms, no published timeline, and no Houthi commitment to stand down — meaning Saudi supply risk could deepen even as the Iran headline risk fades. The next EIA weekly inventory report on Tuesday, July 29 will provide the first hard demand-signal into the de-escalation window.

Geopolitics

US and Iran Announce Mutual Pause in Military Strikes — First Suspension Since July Escalation

The United States and Iran agreed to a mutual pause in military strikes on Sunday July 26, the first halt in near-continuous operations since the ceasefire collapsed in early July. The announcement came after approximately 13 days of sustained US strikes on Iranian military targets. Iran reciprocated, halting its own retaliatory strikes. No formal terms, duration, or timeline were published alongside the pause. Oil markets took the news as a strong bearish signal — Brent briefly dipped below $90 before recovering to ~$92 Monday morning — while equity futures rallied sharply, with S&P 500 futures up ~60 points and Nasdaq futures gaining approximately 1.4%.

Houthis Strike Saudi Aramco's Jizan Refinery and Yanbu Facility in Weekend Escalation

Yemen's Houthis struck two Saudi Aramco facilities over the weekend, escalating their campaign against Saudi oil infrastructure to its most intense level since the 2019 Abqaiq attack. The strikes add a Bab al-Mandeb chokepoint risk on top of the already-constrained Strait of Hormuz and prevented a fuller oil selloff on the Iran pause news — any confirmed damage to Yanbu or Ras Tanura would be an immediate +$5–10 price catalyst.

EIA: US Crude Stocks +2.0 mb to 411.7 mb — Refinery Utilization Hits 96.1%; Next Report Due Tuesday

The EIA's weekly petroleum status report for the week ending July 17, 2026, showed US commercial crude oil inventories rose 2.0 million barrels to 411.7 million barrels, remaining roughly 6% below the five-year seasonal average. Distillate stocks rose 1.4 mb but remain 10% below the five-year average. Refinery utilization hit 96.1% of operable capacity, near operational limits. Crude oil imports averaged 5.8 mb/d — the four-week average is 5.6 mb/d, down 11.4% year-on-year, reflecting the Hormuz disruption. The next report covering the week ending July 25 is due Tuesday, July 29.

OPEC+

OPEC+ Adds 188,000 bpd for August — Saudi Arabia and Russia Each Absorb 62,000 bpd

Seven OPEC+ members agreed on July 5 to raise combined output by 188,000 barrels per day in August 2026, the fifth consecutive monthly increase under the voluntary-cut unwind. Saudi Arabia and Russia each absorb the largest share at 62,000 bpd; Iraq adds 26,000 bpd, Kuwait 16,000 bpd, Kazakhstan 11,000 bpd. Analysts note the quota increase is largely symbolic given the continued Hormuz disruption constrains how much Gulf crude can actually move — transit volumes at ~25 ships per day versus 130 pre-war. The JMMC is scheduled to review progress in August, with members committed to compensating for overproduction since January 2024.

Demand / IEA

IEA: Global Oil Demand Set to Fall 1 mb/d in 2026 — First Annual Decline Since COVID-Era 2020

The IEA's July 2026 Oil Market Report projects global oil demand will decline by approximately 1 million barrels per day in 2026, the first annual contraction since 2020. Global supply rebounded 4.1 mb/d in June to 98.8 mb/d as Hormuz flows partially recovered, but world output remains roughly 9.4 mb/d below pre-war levels. The IEA flags a disconnect between apparently well-supplied crude markets and tight refined product markets — refinery margins hit four-year highs in early July. US retail gasoline averaged $3.78/gallon as of early July, up 20.5% year-on-year, consistent with the IEA's demand-destruction thesis.

The Guru's Verdict — Monday, July 27, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Last week I wrote that the first close above $100 was a warning signal — not because the underlying supply disruption was not real, but because a market trading $30 above its pre-conflict equilibrium on purely military headlines is vulnerable to any hint of de-escalation. That hint arrived Sunday. The US-Iran mutual pause sent Brent down more than 7% in hours, confirming how much war premium had been baked in. Markets were right to be long above $100; they were also right to sell the pause.

My read on today: This is not a clean bearish signal. The US-Iran pause is genuine but lacks the elements that would validate a sustained selloff: no formal terms, no Houthi stand-down, and two Saudi Aramco facilities struck or nearly struck over the weekend. The Jizan fire and Yanbu intercept represent a new front that adds structural floor risk even as the Iran headline premium deflates. The market is not pricing a deal — it is pricing a pause, and pauses can end.

The bigger picture: The risk structure is now the most genuinely two-sided it has been since the war began. The bearish case — an orderly US-Iran drawdown with Hormuz traffic recovering — is the base case. But the bullish tail is fatter than headlines suggest: Houthi capability against Saudi Arabia is demonstrated, spare capacity is thin at roughly 2.5 mb/d, Hormuz transit at 25 ships per day is nowhere near normalization, and the IEA is already projecting the first annual demand decline since 2020 — meaning some demand destruction has already occurred, removing a ceiling on any price recovery.

My call: Brent holds $87–$96 today, with the range anchored by the Jizan/Yanbu floor on the downside and the pause-confidence ceiling on the upside. The war premium is deflating, not gone — and the Houthi factor could re-inflate it overnight.
