Top Story

⛽ Supply Relief — Saudi Petroline Back Online; Brent Breaks Below $100 for the First Time in Weeks

Saudi Arabia Restarts East-West Pipeline After 12-Day Shutdown — Crude Tumbles to September Low as Iran Denies Hormuz Reopening Offer

The first meaningful supply-side development in weeks arrived overnight. Saudi Arabia restarted its 1,200km East-West Pipeline (Petroline) on Wednesday, resuming crude flows from the Persian Gulf toward the Red Sea port of Yanbu after a 12-day shutdown caused by drone attacks attributed to Iraqi militia. Saudi Aramco is working to gradually restore flows toward the pipeline's 4-million-barrel-per-day capacity, with full throughput expected to take several weeks. The restart routes crude around the Strait of Hormuz — and its revival is, for the first time in this war's history, a sign that Saudi Arabia is actively rebuilding its export architecture after months of diversion through Hormuz shuttle operations.

The market's verdict was immediate: Brent crude fell roughly 3.8% to around $97.80 per barrel — its lowest close since September 8 and the first breach below the psychologically significant $100 mark in recent sessions. WTI dropped close to $93.30, also touching a two-week low. Yet the market simultaneously absorbed a sharp setback on the diplomatic front: Iran's semi-official Fars News Agency quickly contradicted a Reuters and Kyodo News report that Tehran had offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days, calling the reports 'invalid and untrue.' The denial extinguished a brief de-escalation rally before it could take hold.

Neither development settles the outlook. The Petroline is currently running well below capacity — Aramco informed European term customers that October allocations via Mediterranean routes would be zero, meaning pipeline crude reaches Yanbu but European refiners see no relief yet. Meanwhile, the IEA's September Oil Market Report underscores the structural depth of the supply crisis: world oil production is averaging 100.7 mb/d in 2026, down 5.7 mb/d year-on-year, with more than 10 mb/d of Gulf output still shut in amid heightened security risks. The Petroline restart is a step, not a resolution.

Today's Key Headlines

01
Saudi Supply

Saudi Arabia's Petroline Back Online — Yanbu Port Exports to Resume After 12-Day Drone-Attack Outage

Saudi Arabia restarted its critical East-West Pipeline on September 22, following drone attacks that forced a shutdown on September 11 and halted crude loadings at the Red Sea port of Yanbu. The 1,200km pipeline can carry up to 4 mb/d of crude from the Gulf to the Red Sea, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. Aramco is restoring flows gradually, with full capacity recovery expected in several weeks. The restart drove Brent crude to its lowest level since September 8, breaking below $100 for the first time in recent sessions. Saudi Arabia had been rerouting crude through Hormuz — loading seven supertankers with 14 million barrels from Gulf terminals over the weekend — ahead of the pipeline's return, with average daily Saudi flows through Hormuz reaching 2.9 mb/d.

Hormuz

Iran Denies Hormuz 7-Day Reopening Offer — Tehran Calls Reuters and Kyodo Reports 'Invalid and Untrue'

Reports on Tuesday from Reuters and Japan's Kyodo News suggested a senior Iranian official had told mediators that Tehran could reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if the United States eased military pressure and lifted its naval blockade on Iranian ports. The reports briefly knocked more than $2 from Brent crude. Within hours, Iran's semi-official Fars News Agency issued a flat denial, saying the claims were 'invalid and untrue.' As of September 23, no confirmed seven-day reopening agreement exists between Iran and the US. The episode highlights how contested and fragile any Hormuz reopening framework remains — with Iranian officials unable or unwilling to confirm diplomatic positions publicly, even as indirect contacts continue through mediators.

EIA Inventories

EIA: US Crude Inventories Drew 1.6 Million Barrels in Week Ending September 18 — Below 2.3 mb Analyst Forecast

The US Energy Information Administration's weekly petroleum status report, released Wednesday September 23, showed commercial crude oil inventories (excluding the Strategic Petroleum Reserve) fell by 1.6 million barrels for the week ending September 18, bringing total commercial stocks to approximately 421.8 million barrels — about 1% above the five-year average. The draw was smaller than the 2.3 million barrel decline analysts had forecast. The American Petroleum Institute's preliminary estimate had pointed to a build of 1.786 million barrels, widening the usual API-EIA divergence. US refinery inputs ran at approximately 17.3 mb/d at 96.8% utilization the prior week, with domestic refiners continuing to process at near-peak rates to meet strained product markets.

IEA / Supply

IEA September OMR: Global Supply Down 5.7 mb/d Year-on-Year — Demand Cut Again; Middle East Recovery Pushed to 2027

The International Energy Agency's September Oil Market Report provided a sobering assessment of the war's ongoing toll. World oil supply is projected to average 100.7 mb/d in 2026, down 5.7 mb/d year-on-year and 1.3 mb/d below last month's already-reduced estimate, with a full recovery in Middle East supplies deferred until 2027. More than 10 mb/d of Gulf output remained shut in during August amid heightened security risks. On the demand side, the IEA revised global oil consumption down 2.5 mb/d for 2026 — 940 kb/d more than the prior estimate — as elevated prices and product shortages weigh on industrial and transport demand, particularly in Asia. The IEA also flagged concentrated losses in middle distillates and petrochemical feedstocks.

06
OPEC+ / Saudi Output

Saudi Crude Production Falls to Lowest Since 1991; OPEC+ September Hike Completes Voluntary Cut Rollback

OPEC's September Monthly Oil Market Report revealed Saudi Arabia's crude production fell to 6.24 mb/d in August — the lowest monthly average since the Gulf War in 1991 — as Houthi maritime attacks and escalating strikes on Red Sea coastal infrastructure forced output and export cuts. OPEC+ as a group added 540,000 b/d month-on-month to reach 28.4 mb/d in September, completing the phased rollback of voluntary cuts agreed in 2023. The group has signalled a pause on further increases in Q4. Iranian crude output also fell to just above 2 mb/d in August — its lowest since 2020. Quota-participating OPEC+ crude output rose 627 kb/d in August on official secondary-source estimates, led by Iraq, which drove higher volumes via aggressive cargo discounting and heavy Hormuz shuttle trade.

The Guru's Verdict — Wednesday, September 23, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

The market needed a supply-side headline and it finally got one: the Saudi Petroline is back online after 12 days dark. Brent is trading around $97.80 as I write this — below $100 for the first time in recent sessions — and the scale of the move tells us how much of the market was pricing in a prolonged Yanbu outage. It was pricing in more than most analysts would have admitted.

My read on today: Don't mistake partial relief for resolution. The Petroline is running at a fraction of its 4 mb/d capacity, European October allocations from Yanbu are still zero, and Iran's swift denial of the Reuters Hormuz offer removed the one diplomatic catalyst that could have driven a sustained move below $95. We are not in a recovery — we are in a brief pause in the tightening that has characterized this market since February.

The bigger picture: The IEA's September OMR delivered the clearest scorecard yet: world supply is down 5.7 mb/d year-on-year, more than 10 mb/d of Gulf output is still shut in, and a full Middle East supply recovery has been pushed out to 2027. Saudi Arabia produced just 6.24 mb/d in August — its lowest since the Gulf War in 1991. A pipeline restart at reduced capacity is a data point, not a market turn.

My call: Brent holds the $95–$103 range today, biased lower on the Saudi pipeline news but with explosive upside potential if any UNGA bilateral signal materialises. Trump has told the market the deal comes 'right after the election' — which means the next seven weeks are structurally supported by premium, with volatile sessions whenever diplomacy flickers. The floor has not moved. The ceiling has just become harder to reach.
