Top Story

⚡ Escalation Resumes — First US Strike on Iran in a Month Ends Fragile Diplomatic Pause

US Forces Strike Iranian Rocket Launchers on Larak Island — Brent Surges Above $90 as Hormuz Peace Hopes Fade

A fragile month-long pause in US–Iran military hostilities collapsed over the weekend when American forces struck two IRGC rocket launchers on Larak Island in the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday, August 30. US intelligence had observed Iranian forces preparing to deploy sea mines into the waterway — a development the Pentagon said it could not allow to proceed. The strike — the first US military action against Iran in approximately one month — immediately erased what had been a brief window of diplomatic optimism: Oman-mediated back-channel talks between Washington and Tehran had suggested a possible easing of conditions for the strait's reopening. Brent surged above $90 per barrel in Monday morning trading, posting its largest single-day gain in three weeks.

Iran's Foreign Ministry had already reiterated over the weekend that the Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly one-fifth of global seaborne oil flows in normal times — would not reopen until US sanctions were fully lifted and Washington paid war reparations. Tanker transits through the corridor have fallen from approximately 130 per day before the February 28 outbreak of hostilities to as few as 6–15 transits daily. The EIA has described this as "the largest energy supply disruption in recorded history." Sunday's strike has effectively reset the diplomatic clock to zero.

The Larak escalation compounds a structural fragility in global oil supply that has been building since February. OPEC+ this month completed its final planned production quota increases for 2026 — reversing all 3.5 million bpd of 2023 cuts — but the move is largely symbolic while physical barrels from Middle Eastern producers cannot transit Hormuz. Adding to the cartel's existential pressures, Venezuela is reported to be weighing an exit from OPEC, with the Trump administration said to be in talks about acquiring a US stake in the country's oil resources. If confirmed, the combined departures of Venezuela and the UAE — which withdrew earlier in 2026 — would strip OPEC of roughly 5 million bpd of production capacity, approximately 17% of core member supply.

Venezuela Weighs OPEC Exit as Trump Administration Eyes US Stake in Its Oil Fields

Venezuela is examining plans to leave OPEC — the cartel it helped found in 1960 — Bloomberg reported, as the Trump administration holds talks with Venezuela's interim government about acquiring a US stake in the country's approximately 1.16 mb/d oil resource base. No final decision has been made, but the potential exit follows the UAE's earlier 2026 withdrawal. The two combined departures would reduce OPEC's core member production capacity by roughly 5 million bpd, or about 17%, and would accelerate the cartel's structural erosion at a moment when it is already struggling to maintain relevance amid the Hormuz crisis.

OPEC+ Supply

OPEC+ Ratifies Final Planned 2026 Output Hike — 188,000 bpd for September

At its August 2 ministerial meeting, OPEC+ ratified a 188,000 bpd production quota increase for September, completing the full reversal of the 3.5 million bpd in cuts implemented in 2023. The seven participating members — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — are expected to hold quotas steady through year-end. Analysts were broadly skeptical: while the increases are real on paper, physical barrels from most Middle Eastern OPEC+ producers remain blocked by the Hormuz crisis, meaning the supply boost exists mainly in quota ledgers, not in actual shipments.

The Guru's Verdict — Monday, August 31, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Sunday's Larak Island strike is, in a single word, destabilizing — not necessarily in the sense that prices go vertical from here, but in the specific and important sense that it has dismantled the one thin thread of optimism that had held since late July: the idea that Oman could quietly broker an off-ramp. That thread is now cut. The Hormuz reopening is not a near-term story; it is at minimum a 2027 story, and possibly longer.

My read on today: Brent above $90 at Monday's open is the market pricing something real, not just noise. The Larak strike was not a minor skirmish — it was the US destroying hardware that Iran was actively preparing to use to extend the mining of the most important oil-shipping lane on the planet. Iran's formal response almost certainly cannot be softer than its pre-strike posture, and could easily be harder. The risk is deeply asymmetric: the downside from here is $2–3/bbl if Tehran backs down; the upside if it retaliates against a tanker is $8–15/bbl.

The bigger picture: We enter September with three overlapping structural pressures on crude supply: first, a Strait of Hormuz that is no closer to reopening and may now be further away; second, an OPEC+ completing paper quota increases it cannot physically deliver; and third, an incipient crisis of legitimacy at the cartel itself, with Venezuela likely to follow the UAE toward the exit. None of these are short-term catalysts — all three are directionally bullish for the long-run price floor.

My call: Brent trades $88–$96 today, with the most probable range $90–$93 absent an immediate Iranian counter-strike. The geopolitical risk premium is back. It never fully left — it just slept for a month. Today it woke up.
