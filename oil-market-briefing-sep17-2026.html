Top Story

⚡ Pipeline Watch — EIA Confirms Draw as Aramco Races to Restart Bypass Route

EIA Quashes the API Illusion: −0.6 mb Draw Confirmed — Brent Softens to $107.77 as Aramco Targets Half-Pipeline Capacity Within Days

Wednesday delivered both the resolution traders had been waiting for and the news that the market's next battle has already begun. The EIA weekly petroleum status report for the week ending September 11 confirmed a commercial crude draw of −0.6 million barrels — squarely contradicting the API's overnight build of +7.14 mb that had unsettled markets on Tuesday. As this column flagged yesterday, the API figure was almost certainly a mechanical artifact: pre-shutdown Yanbu export volumes loaded before the September 11 drone strike arrived at US import terminals mid-week, inflating crude stock numbers without reflecting forward supply conditions. The EIA has now put the ghost to rest.

Simultaneously, Saudi Aramco moved to contain the East-West pipeline crisis. State-run Aramco is bypassing the damaged section of the 1,200-km conduit and targets restoring roughly half the pipeline's 4–5 mb/d capacity within days, with full restoration expected in approximately six weeks. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright had signalled Tuesday that the outage should last "only a matter of days," lending diplomatic weight to Aramco's engineering timeline. Brent eased from Wednesday's close of $108.75 to $107.77 in early Thursday trading — a −0.90% retreat that reflects restart optimism rather than any change in the underlying supply picture, which remains historically stressed.

Libya added a moment of relief to an otherwise grim week. The National Oil Corporation confirmed that production at El Feel, Wafa, and other shuttered fields has resumed after the Petroleum Facilities Guard stood down following an agreement to begin negotiations over transferring the force's administrative oversight to the NOC. The restoration removes the Libya force-majeure overlay that compounded Tuesday's supply anxiety, though it does nothing to address the far larger structural problem: more than 10 mb/d of Gulf output remains shut in, the East-West pipeline is still at half-capacity at best, and the Strait of Hormuz remains disrupted for the eighth consecutive month.

Today's Key Headlines

01
EIA Inventory

EIA Confirms −0.6 mb Crude Draw — The API's +7.14 mb Build Was a Pre-Shutdown Cargo Illusion

The EIA's weekly petroleum status report for the week ending September 11 showed commercial crude inventories fell 640,000 barrels to 423.4 million barrels — 1% above the five-year average. The draw directly contradicted the API's reported +7.14 million barrel build, which analysts now attribute to Yanbu pre-shutdown export cargoes landing at US ports mid-week. Distillate fuel stocks fell further and are forecast to breach the critical 100-million-barrel floor before quarter-end, a threshold not crossed since 2014. Refinery utilisation held at 96.8% of operable capacity.

02
Saudi Supply

Aramco Bypassing Damaged Section — Half the East-West Pipeline's Capacity Targeted Within Days

Saudi Aramco is engineering a bypass around the section of the East-West pipeline damaged in last Thursday's drone strike, which knocked out 4–5 million barrels per day of export capacity — approximately 4% of global supply. The company is targeting roughly half-capacity resumption within days, with full restoration projected over six weeks. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright confirmed the restart timeline Tuesday, though crude loadings at the Red Sea hub of Yanbu remain reduced and some late-September European cargo deliveries have been cancelled. Goldman Sachs has put a probability on Brent exceeding $120/bbl if repairs stretch to the eight-week upper-bound scenario.

03
Libya

Libya Restores El Feel, Wafa and Three Other Fields After Petroleum Guards Stand Down

The National Oil Corporation confirmed that production has resumed at El Feel, Wafa, and several other suspended fields after the Petroleum Facilities Guard ended its valve-closure protest. The PFG, which had shut the Hamada-Zawiya pipeline and threatened force majeure, stood down after the prime minister's office agreed to begin formal negotiations on transferring the security force from the Defence Ministry to the NOC. Libya was producing approximately 1.1 mb/d before the disruption; the NOC said operations have returned to normal, removing one of three concurrent supply flashpoints from the market — at least for now.

IEA Outlook

IEA September OMR: World Supply −5.7 mb/d Year-on-Year; Full Gulf Recovery Deferred to 2027

The IEA's September Oil Market Report cut its 2026 global oil demand forecast by a further 940,000 b/d (to a decline of 2.5 mb/d year-on-year) as the US–Iran impasse delays normalisation of flows. World oil supply is projected to average 100.7 mb/d in 2026, down 5.7 mb/d year-on-year, with August production falling another 1.6 mb/d month-on-month to 100.1 mb/d as more than 10 mb/d of Gulf output remains shut in. A full supply recovery — estimated at 8 mb/d — is now forecast for 2027. Middle distillate stocks are highlighted as the most acute product market risk, with global diesel inventories at their lowest level since 2014.

05
OPEC+

OPEC+ Holds October Output at September Levels — Saudi Production Hits Lowest Since 1990

The seven core OPEC+ nations — Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria, and Oman — voted on September 6 to maintain required October 2026 production at September levels, completing the phased rollback of the 2023 voluntary cuts. Saudi Arabia's quota is largely theoretical: Aramco production has slumped to its lowest level since 1990 as export routes via Hormuz remain disrupted and the East-West pipeline is now also offline. OPEC total output ran at approximately 28.4 mb/d in August, with Saudi Arabia accounting for the single largest shortfall. The group's next meeting is scheduled for October 4, 2026.

Houthi Risk

Houthis Intensify Saudi Strikes — Bab el-Mandab Transit Risk Rises as Red Sea Lanes Narrow

Iran-backed Houthi forces launched renewed drone and missile strikes on Saudi Arabia this week, including attacks on Khamis Mushait, Abha, and Taif, while maintaining a threat posture over the Bab el-Mandab Strait — the 18-mile chokepoint through which the East-West pipeline's Yanbu exports must transit to reach European and Asian buyers. With Yanbu loadings suspended and the strait increasingly contested, Saudi crude now lacks a clear reliable export route. Analysts at gCaptain and DTN note that US crude shipping costs to Asia have hit a record $44.8 million per VLCC, as buyers scramble for alternatives.

The Guru's Verdict — Thursday, September 17, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Yesterday I called $104–$112 biased two-sided, with the EIA print as the twin catalyst alongside the Yanbu clock. Brent held $107.98 at the close on Wednesday, entirely within the range. The EIA delivered the better outcome: a confirmed −0.6 mb draw rather than the API's +7.14 mb phantom build, and crude barely flinched — which tells you everything about where the market's attention actually sits. It's not inventory mechanics right now. It's the pipeline.

My read on today: The API ghost story is buried. The real September 17 question is whether Aramco's bypass engineers can deliver visible barrels within the "days" window that both the company and the US Energy Secretary have publicly committed to. Crude at $107.77 is priced for a successful bypass — not a fast one, but a believable one. If Aramco confirms partial flows before the end of the day or tomorrow, Brent drifts toward $104. If there's any slippage in the activation timeline, the Goldman Sachs >$120 call comes back to the front page immediately.

The bigger structural picture: Distillates are the shadow crisis that crude headlines obscure. The gasoil crack spread has been running at five times its pre-crisis level — roughly $100/bbl — for weeks. US distillate stocks are forecast to breach 100 million barrels this quarter for the first time since 2014. You cannot fix a distillate shortage by hoping Saudi crude gets through a bypass; you need functioning refineries in the Gulf, which requires Hormuz, which requires a diplomatic breakthrough that remains as elusive as ever. The crack spread is the more honest price signal right now than Brent itself.

My call: Brent trades $104–$111 today, with a downside bias on bypass confirmation and a sharp upside skip above $112 if Aramco's engineering runs into trouble or Houthis launch a new strike on Yanbu terminal infrastructure. The pipeline bypass is the only event horizon that matters today. Everything else is commentary.
