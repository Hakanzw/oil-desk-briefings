⚡ Workaround Works — For Now: Aramco's Ship-to-Ship Bridge Trims the Crisis Premium

Brent Falls to $103.59 in Third Straight Session as Aramco's 20 mb Ship-to-Ship Lifeline Eases Asia Supply Fears — But Hormuz, Houthis, and the Pipeline Clock Persist

For three consecutive sessions, oil has done something it had not managed since the East-West pipeline went dark on September 11: it has fallen. Brent crude retreated to $103.59 on Friday, shedding a further 1.17% as markets absorbed the clearest evidence yet that Saudi Aramco has found a workable — if costly and fragile — near-term answer to its Red Sea export problem. Having lost the 4–5 mb/d East-West pipeline to last week's drone strikes, Aramco has pivoted hard: the company has offered approximately 20 million barrels to Asian refiners this week alone, loaded via ship-to-ship transfers off Sohar in Oman and Fujairah in the UAE — both outside the contested Strait of Hormuz. Shuttle tankers carry Saudi crude through Hormuz, hand it to the buyer's vessel waiting in calmer waters, and the buyer sails home without entering the strait. It is improvised logistics, not a structural fix, but it is real supply reaching real buyers, and the market is pricing that.

Yet the retreat in Brent must not be mistaken for resolution. The three-session slide from $108.75 on Wednesday to $103.59 today represents the evaporation of the panic premium built into crude after last Thursday's attack — not the removal of the underlying supply deficit. Aramco's ship-to-ship operation is expensive, slow, and exposes both shuttle and receiving vessels to ongoing Houthi risk in Hormuz transit. The East-West pipeline bypass, which targets roughly half of the conduit's 4–5 mb/d capacity, had still not been publicly confirmed operational as of Friday morning. And the Strait of Hormuz itself — through which roughly 20% of global seaborne oil must ultimately pass to reach the open ocean — remains effectively restricted for the ninth consecutive month.

Geopolitics offered the week's most significant wild card on Thursday afternoon: Reuters reported, citing three Iranian sources, that China has privately pressed Tehran to use its influence over the Houthis to restrain further attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure. Riyadh turned to Beijing — the world's largest crude importer — after Houthi forces intensified pressure on the Bab el-Mandab Strait, threatening to disrupt the very Red Sea corridor on which Aramco's ship-to-ship workaround depends. Iran's response was non-committal, but the contact is significant: it is the first reported direct Chinese mediation attempt since the war began in February. And on Thursday, President Trump confirmed he would meet the six Gulf Cooperation Council leaders at the United Nations General Assembly in New York on September 22 to discuss the Iran war endgame and postwar reconstruction — the first structured multilateral diplomatic summit since June.

01
Saudi Workaround

Aramco Sells ~20 mb to Asian Refiners via Ship-to-Ship — The New Export Bridge Outside Hormuz

Saudi Aramco has moved aggressively to offset the closure of the East-West pipeline by offering Arab Medium and Arab Heavy crude via ship-to-ship transfers off Sohar (Oman) and Fujairah (UAE) for a second consecutive week, selling approximately 20 million barrels to Asian buyers — primarily Chinese state-owned and independent refiners. Shuttle tankers load at the Gulf terminals, transit the Strait of Hormuz, and pass the cargo to the buyer's vessel waiting in open water, eliminating the need for Asian refiners to send their own ships into Hormuz. The operation provides near-term supply relief and is the primary driver of Brent's three-session decline. Analysts caution that the workaround is expensive, operationally slow, and vulnerable to any Houthi escalation in Hormuz or the Sohar approach channels.

Diplomacy

China Presses Iran to Rein In Houthis — Beijing Acts on Saudi Appeal for Red Sea Calm

China privately asked Tehran to use its influence over Yemen's Houthis to restrain further attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure, Reuters reported Thursday, citing three Iranian sources. Riyadh appealed to Beijing after Houthi forces intensified strikes on Saudi territory and threatened the Bab el-Mandab Strait — the narrow chokepoint through which Aramco's ship-to-ship workaround must ultimately export crude. Beijing, which imports roughly 40% of its crude from the Gulf, has a direct economic interest in Red Sea stability. Iran's response was non-committal, with officials saying regional peace depends on ending the US-Israel war. The contact nonetheless marks the first direct Chinese mediation attempt since the conflict began in February 2026, and adds a new diplomatic track to an otherwise militarily dominated week.

Geopolitics

Trump to Meet Gulf Leaders at UNGA September 22 — Iran War Endgame and Postwar Strategy on Agenda

President Trump will meet the six Gulf Cooperation Council leaders — Saudi Arabia, UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman — on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York on September 22, Axios reported. The summit may also include other Arab and Muslim leaders. Discussions are expected to focus on US ideas for a postwar Iran strategy, with the Trump administration's broader "day-after" plan expected to be finalised after November's midterm elections. Trump said Thursday the US was "hopefully nearing the end" of the war with Iran. The UNGA summit represents the first structured multilateral diplomatic session on the Iran war endgame since the June ceasefire framework collapsed in August, and is the most significant diplomatic catalyst on the near-term oil market calendar.

EIA Outlook

EIA September STEO: Brent to Average $90/bbl in H2 2026, Falling to $74 in 2027 as Supply Recovers

The EIA's September 2026 Short-Term Energy Outlook projects Brent crude will average $90/bbl in the second half of 2026 before declining to $74/bbl in 2027 as Middle East oil production gradually recovers and inventories rebuild. US crude output is forecast to reach 13.8 mb/d in 2026 and 14.3 mb/d in 2027. The EIA notes that "constraints to exporting oil from the Middle East will persist through the end of the year, keeping crude oil production in the region below pre-conflict averages until Q2 2027." Diesel retail prices are forecast at $5.07/gallon for 2026 — the highest annual average in the EIA's modern dataset. Natural gas storage is projected to reach 3,969 billion cubic feet by October 31, 5% above the five-year average, as LNG export demand and power-sector generation compete for supply.

Pipeline Watch

East-West Bypass Still Unconfirmed — The Single Confirmation the Market Needs Most

As of Friday morning, Saudi Aramco has not publicly confirmed that its bypass around the September 11-damaged section of the East-West pipeline is operational. The company's target remains roughly half the pipeline's 4–5 mb/d capacity within days, with full restoration in approximately six weeks — a timeline first stated on September 16 and reiterated by US Energy Secretary Chris Wright. The longer confirmation takes, the more the market allocates weight to the ship-to-ship workaround as the primary supply mechanism — a workaround that itself carries Houthi transit risk through Hormuz. Any public Aramco statement confirming even partial pipeline flows would be the single most bearish near-term catalyst available. Continued silence through the weekend would be incrementally bullish.

Distillates

ICE Low Sulphur Gasoil (ULS1!) Falls 3.23% to $1,491.25/mt — Crack Still at Structural Crisis Levels

ICE Europe Low Sulphur Gasoil futures (ULS1!) fell 3.23% to $1,491.25 per metric tonne on Friday — the largest single-session decline in the distillate complex since the pipeline crisis began. The drop, far steeper than Brent's 1.17% retreat, reflects easing near-term fears as Aramco's workaround buys some time for refineries still processing Gulf crudes. But the underlying structural picture has not changed: the gasoil/diesel crack spread against Brent remains near ~$97/bbl, roughly five times its pre-crisis average. EIA retail diesel is forecast at $5.07/gallon for 2026 — the highest annual average on record. The distillate market's message is consistent: product tightness is supply-chain deep and is not resolved by a ship-to-ship operation that delivers crude, not finished product.

The Guru's Verdict — Friday, September 18, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Yesterday I called $104–$111 biased lower on bypass confirmation. Brent tested the $102–$103 range in Thursday's session — breaking below the call's lower bound — before settling around $103 as ship-to-ship supply news continued to circulate. Today's $103.59 open sits just inside the lower end of that range. The call was directionally right: the pipeline bypass has not been confirmed, but Aramco's workaround delivered enough of a supply signal to drag crude below $104. I'll take that as a rough vindication, but this is not the all-clear.

My read on today: The market is now caught between two supply narratives — the workaround (real but fragile) and the bypass (targeted but unconfirmed). Brent at $103.59 has shed roughly 40% of the panic premium that built after September 11, which is approximately right: the workaround is genuine, but approximately 60% of the structural supply problem remains unresolved. Hormuz is still restricted. The pipeline is still offline. The Houthis are still operational in Bab el-Mandab. This is not a market that has resolved its supply problem. It has found a workaround — and workarounds have a way of breaking precisely when markets trust them most.

The new variable: The diplomatic calendar suddenly matters in a way it has not since June. The Trump–GCC summit at the UN on September 22 is the first structured multilateral conversation on the Iran war endgame since the ceasefire framework collapsed. China's reported pressure on Iran to restrain the Houthis adds a second diplomatic track running in parallel. Neither is a resolution. But together they introduce a probability mass that wasn't there a week ago: a scenario where the June framework is reopened and a Hormuz normalisation timeline is back on the table. That probability was close to zero last Friday. It is not zero today.

My call: Brent trades $101–$108 today, with a neutral-to-downside bias if the bypass confirms and the ship-to-ship operation holds through the weekend, and a sharp upside skip to $110+ if any Houthi strike hits the transfer corridor off Sohar or Fujairah. The workaround is the new pipeline. Everything that follows depends on whether the Houthis let it stand.
