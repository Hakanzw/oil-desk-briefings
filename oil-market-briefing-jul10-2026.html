Top Story

Islamabad MOU Is Dead in All But Name — Trump Declares Ceasefire "Over"; US Strikes Kharg Island; Seizure Threat Targets 90% of Iran's Crude Exports; Brent Holds Near $77 as Markets Price the Worst

Trump Says Ceasefire Is "Over" After US Strikes Kharg Island — IRGC Retaliates Against 85 US Bases; Kharg Seizure Would Remove 2.5–3 mb/d From Global Supply; Brent Surged 5%+ Wednesday, Holds $76.57 Friday

Three weeks after the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding was signed on June 17, the ceasefire it codified is effectively dead. Speaking from the NATO summit in Ankara on July 8, President Trump declared: "I think it's over. I don't want to deal with them anymore; they're scum." He revealed that US forces had struck Kharg Island — Iran's primary crude export hub — the previous night, and threatened further escalation: "Maybe we'll take over Kharg Island, and we may take over Kharg Island, and there's not a thing they can do about it." Those words hit the oil tape like a detonation. Brent surged more than 5% on Wednesday as traders priced the scenario that US military forces might seize or permanently neutralize the island that handles approximately 90% of Iran's crude oil exports — roughly 950 million barrels annually — and through which China, India, and South Korea receive the bulk of their Iranian crude. US CENTCOM separately confirmed it had struck approximately 90 Iranian military targets in the July 8 operation, including air defense systems, coastal surveillance assets, missile and drone storage sites, and IRGC naval capabilities along Iran's coastline. It was the second consecutive day of US strikes on Iran following CENTCOM's initial operation on July 7 in response to the Hormuz tanker attacks on three commercial vessels.

Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps responded by striking 85 US military sites in Bahrain and Kuwait, and Iranian leadership accused Washington of violating the Islamabad MOU's core provisions. The IRGC issued a warning that it would "retaliate in a harsher way" in the coming days — a statement that markets are pricing as a near-certainty given the escalatory cycle now underway. Kharg Island's strategic significance cannot be overstated. Located approximately 55 kilometers off Iran's coast in the Persian Gulf, the island's deep-water terminals serve as the pipeline terminus for multiple Iranian offshore fields, and it handles essentially all of Iran's crude export volumes. During the Iran-Iraq War of the 1980s, Iraqi bombing of Kharg was the primary lever used to throttle Iranian oil revenue. A US seizure in 2026 would be categorically more severe: it would represent a permanent, kinetic removal of Iran's ability to export crude — not a temporary disruption, but a structural elimination of 2.5–3 million barrels per day of supply from global markets. Brent's 5% surge on Wednesday reflected only the initial premium assigned to that scenario. If US forces moved from striking Kharg to occupying it, the tape would move another $15–20/bbl from current levels.

Amid the escalating military exchange, two data points arrived that complicate the supply narrative. The EIA's July 8 weekly petroleum report — covering the week ending July 3 — showed the first crude inventory build in weeks: +2.998 million barrels, against a forecast decline of 1.9 million barrels. Stocks stand at 411.4 million barrels, 6% below the five-year seasonal average. The build confirms that Gulf crude was reaching US shores even before the re-escalation — meaning the supply pipeline was normalizing precisely when the political architecture collapsed. That data has been almost entirely displaced by the geopolitical shock. Separately, natural gas diverged sharply from crude: Henry Hub front-month futures dropped more than 6% Thursday to a six-week low of approximately $3.01, held near $2.99 Friday — a move driven by two supply-specific factors entirely unrelated to the Iranian crisis. Freeport LNG, one of the largest US liquefied natural gas export facilities, began maintenance on pre-treatment and liquefaction facilities today, July 10, continuing through late August, sharply reducing feedgas demand. A 61 Bcf natural gas storage injection for the week ending July 3 far exceeded the 49 Bcf consensus forecast, widening the inventory surplus. The oil-gas divergence tells a layered story: crude markets are pricing Iranian geopolitical risk while gas markets are pricing domestic supply-demand fundamentals. That divergence will close only when the Iranian situation resolves — in one direction or the other.

The Guru's Verdict — Friday, July 10, 2026

Oil Trading Intelligence — Independent Market Opinion

Three weeks ago, the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding was the most bullish-for-peace event in the oil market since February 28. This column tracked the sequential de-risking: Brent fell from $144 in April to $71.89 by July 7, with five consecutive OPEC+ hikes, Saudi Arabia's record OSP cuts, and the IEA's surplus forecast all pointing toward a structural bear case. On July 5 I wrote: "The August 21 waiver expiry is priced at zero risk; any event that raises that probability produces a $5–8/bbl shock from a tape with no cushion." Two days later the Hormuz tanker attacks arrived. Two days after that, Trump declared the ceasefire "over," US forces struck Kharg Island, and the president threatened to seize the hub through which 90% of Iran's crude exports flow. Brent has moved from $73.29 (Wednesday's briefing) to $76.57 today — a 4.5% gain over two sessions. That is the opening adjustment in a market that is trying, and largely failing, to price three wildly different outcomes from a single set of facts.

My read on the current situation: The ceasefire is not simply "stressed" — it is functionally suspended. But it is not definitively dead, and the distinction matters enormously for where Brent trades next week. Trump's paradox — declaring the ceasefire "over" while allowing negotiators to continue talking — is either the most sophisticated maximum-pressure campaign of his presidency or genuine policy incoherence. If it is a pressure campaign, the Islamabad MOU can be restored within 48–72 hours of Iranian concessions, and Brent would give back $4–6 as bear fundamentals reassert. If it reflects a genuine shift toward Iranian regime pressure or military resolution, Brent has another $15–20 to move higher as Kharg Island seizure probability approaches certainty. The market is at $76.57 because it cannot distinguish between those two scenarios. It will be forced to do so within 72 hours as Iranian signals clarify.

What has structurally changed since the last briefing: Kharg Island is now a live military objective. That is categorically different from anything priced in the prior six weeks. Saudi Arabia's $11/bbl OSP cut and OPEC+'s five consecutive hikes were both predicated on Iranian crude flowing freely through the Strait of Hormuz. A Kharg Island seizure removes the supply entirely — not by blocking the Strait, but by eliminating Iran's ability to load crude onto tankers regardless of whether the Strait is navigable. The EIA's first crude build (+3.0M bbl, week ending July 3) is almost irrelevant against this backdrop except as a baseline: it confirms supply was normalizing before the collapse, which means a rapid diplomatic resolution would see that normalization resume quickly. The natural gas divergence — six-week low on Freeport maintenance while crude holds near $77 — is the one clean domestic data signal: the commodity complex is not in a generalized risk-on move; it is pricing a specifically Iranian supply shock.

My call: Brent $74–$84 for the week, with the range driven entirely by Kharg Island's fate and the diplomatic back-channel's viability. Rapid ceasefire restoration via pressure gambit: $70–$73, bear trend reasserts, OPEC+ hike cycle resumes, Brent tests the low $70s. Kharg Island degraded but not seized, sustained military pressure: $76–$80, geopolitical premium holds at current levels. Kharg Island seized or Iranian supply structurally disrupted: $84–$92, six-week rally, worst-case $90+ if Hormuz access threatened concurrently. Friday's $76.57 is the maximum-uncertainty price. It will not hold — resolution, in either direction, is imminent and violent. The only position that makes sense at this price is one where you know which outcome you're betting on.
